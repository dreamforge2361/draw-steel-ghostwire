# The Neon Shambles

*The Flats // Ossian Reach // Night-Market Core*

## The Long Bazaar

*Covered stall-lane, everything for sale*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

The lane finds you before you find it — a seam of gold light bleeding between two leaning tenement-stacks, and then the crowd just swallows you whole. The Long Bazaar has no beginning and no end that anyone agrees on; it simply is, a covered market-street that snakes for the better part of a kilometer under forty stories of stacked signage, each floor cantilevered a little further out than the one below until the "sky" — the underside of District 1's foundations — presses down close enough that the tallest stall-poles scrape it. Chemical rain drips through a thousand patch-welded seams in the cover-plating above, and the vendors have long since stopped fighting it: every stall has its own gutter-rig, its own oily tarp, its own private drizzle running off into the gutters in ropes of neon-stained water.

Overhead, in the meter of open air between the cover-plating and the tenement walls, courier-drones weave in tight formation past a hovercab discharging passengers onto a gantry-stair, while below your boots a cargo-crawler the width of the lane forces the whole crowd to flow around it like water around a stone, its driver leaning on the horn in a language of pure irritation. The air is a wall you walk into: frying oil and ozone, six tongues shouted over each other, a hundred barkers competing with the sub-bass thrum that never quite goes away down here. Signs stack on signs stack on signs, pink over gold over sodium-white, until the glow pools on the wet stone like something spilled.

Somewhere in that press, a hand you never saw brushes your pocket and is gone before you can turn.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**Old Madam Ferrin** — runs the Bazaar's unofficial stall-lottery; a Corran guild-clan matriarch who has traded space in this lane for forty years and remembers every debt owed on it. Wants the lane's peace kept more than she wants any single deal.

**Ticker** — a teenage runner for a dozen different stall-owners simultaneously, carrying messages, small goods, and warnings faster than the Wired can flag them. Knows every blind spot in the covering.

**Barrow-Eyes Sudek** — a Nyx-connected fence who runs three stalls as fronts for moving grey-tech; affable, quotes prices in three currencies at once, always watching the crowd over your shoulder.

#### Security

Zone: Flats — nominally lawless, but Old Madam Ferrin's stall-lottery functions as a soft government; violence disrupts commerce and commerce is the only law anyone respects here.

No visible patrols. Trouble is handled by whoever's stall it happens near — a dozen vendors keep clubs, stun-batons, or worse under the counter, and a scuffle draws help from every direction in seconds, whether you want it or not. Drawing a gun escalates fast: stall-owners will flood the lane to protect stock, effectively mobbing the offender. **Alert** here means the crowd itself turns hostile.

Pickpockets and cutpurses (see The Crossing, below) treat the Bazaar as open hunting ground; Ferrin tolerates it as a tax on tourists but not on regulars.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

A blizzard of overlapping AR price-tags, translation overlays, and Obsidian ad-spam so dense that newcomers' AR filters choke on it; locals run heavy filter-scripts just to see the actual stalls underneath.

SIN-readers are sparse and mostly cosmetic — payment kiosks scan chips, but half the transactions in the lane are cash-and-carry, deliberately off the Wired's books.

A hacker who digs past the ad-noise finds Ferrin's private lottery-ledger broadcasting on a hidden channel, encoding which stalls are fronts, which are legitimate, and which owe her favors — a goldmine for anyone planning to move product through here unnoticed.

#### Plot Hooks

1. The Vanishing Stall. A stall that's operated in the same spot for twenty years disappears overnight — tarp, goods, and owner gone, replaced by silence and a fresh weld where the pole was. The crew is hired to find out who erased them, and why Ferrin won't discuss it.

2. Grey-Tech Under the Counter. Barrow-Eyes Sudek needs a shipment of hot cyberware moved through the Bazaar before a Caduceus audit sweep hits the district — good pay, but the ware is stolen from a war-effort medical convoy, and someone up the chain wants it back quietly.

3. Ferrin's Debt. Old Madam Ferrin calls in a favor: a Ferrum rent-collector has started "inspecting" stalls for code violations that don't exist, a slow squeeze meant to clear the lane for redevelopment. The crew is asked to make the inspections stop — without starting a war Ferrin can't win.

## Simsense Row

*Obsidian dream-dens & chip parlors*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

The pink finds you a full block before the row does — a wash of candy-light bleeding around a corner so tight you have to turn sideways, and then it opens up into a lane that seems to exhale warmth and want at you all at once. Simsense Row is barely wide enough for two people to pass, its low ceiling of scavenged awnings and dead ductwork strung with a thousand chip-parlor signs in looping obsidian script, each one promising a different flavor of not-being-yourself for an hour. There is no sky here, only the underside of the level above sagging low between the sign-mounts, and the rain that finds its way through runs pink and cyan by the time it reaches the ground, dripping off cable bundles like something bleeding light.

Above the alley's narrow throat, in the sliver of open air the buildings leave, a lone drone hangs motionless, camera-eye fixed on the door of the row's oldest den, while ground level is choked with idling patrons too spun on chip-dreams to notice the cargo-sled trying to squeeze past them, its rigger leaning out to shove a shoulder clear. The sound wall here has its own texture — not the barking-vendor roar of the Bazaar but a low murmur of a hundred half-conscious sighs leaking from doorway curtains, sales-pitches whispered instead of shouted, synth-lullabies bleeding out of speaker grilles tuned just under the threshold of comfortable.

A woman in an Obsidian-branded coat catches your eye through a beaded curtain and smiles like she already knows exactly what you're missing.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**Mother Solvay** — den-mother of the row's largest parlor, The Amber Hour; Obsidian-licensed but runs a genuinely humane operation by Row standards, screening chips for lethal loops. Protects her regulars fiercely.

**Chip-Doctor Renn** — a semi-legal splicer who cuts custom simsense from stolen memory-fragments; brilliant, unstable, always one bad edit from burning out a customer's mind for good.

**Vex** — an Obsidian enforcer posing as a doorman, quietly cataloguing which dens skim off unlicensed BTL chips so corporate can shake them down later.

#### Security

Zone: Flats, under loose Obsidian licensing — the corp doesn't police the row directly but taxes it through "protection" fees collected by enforcers like Vex.

Most violence is internal — den-owners settling disputes over customers or territory — and gets absorbed quietly; nobody wants Obsidian auditors drawn by a body count. Overt gunplay brings Obsidian muscle fast and hard, since dead customers are bad advertising.

BTL (bootleg simsense) dealers work the shadows between legit dens; a bad batch burning out a mind is common enough that it barely draws notice unless the victim was somebody.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

Obsidian's AR licensing tags glow over every legitimate doorway — green for clean chips, amber for "experimental," and a conspicuous absence of tags over the BTL dens, which prefer to stay dark.

SIN-readers log every purchase at licensed parlors (Obsidian likes its data), which makes the row a poor place to be discreet — unless you're buying from the unlicensed stalls, which is exactly why they exist.

A hacker digging into the row's local node-cluster finds Vex's private surveillance feed — a map of which dens are skimming, which is either leverage or a death sentence depending on who's asking.

#### Plot Hooks

1. The Amber Hour's Problem. Mother Solvay's newest supplier is sourcing chips built from stolen combat-veteran memories — pulled straight from the war climbing the foundations. She wants the crew to find out who's harvesting soldiers' minds before Obsidian corporate notices and burns the row down to cover it up.

2. Renn's Masterpiece. Chip-Doctor Renn has cut something new — a simsense chip that isn't a memory at all, but a message, encoded by a Wired-touched source the crew might recognize as a Technomancer. Someone dangerous wants it destroyed unplayed.

3. Vex's Ledger. Vex is quietly building a case against a rival Obsidian enforcer skimming protection money. He'll pay the crew to steal the rival's books — but the books also implicate a Sanctum actuary laundering risk-data through the row, and that's a much bigger fish.

## The Crossing

*Five-lane knot; pickpocket king's court*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

You feel the Crossing before you see it — the crowd's flow changes, currents merging from five directions at once into a knot of humanity so dense it has its own weather. Five market-lanes meet here in a space too small for what it holds, an irregular hub where the ceiling opens just enough to reveal three more levels of gantry stacked above, chemical rain sheeting down through the gap in visible curtains lit gold and rose by the signage crowding every surface. A courier-drone dives through the gap in a tight spiral, threading between a hovercab crawling nose-to-tail with ground traffic and a boosted rig whose driver has clearly given up and is just leaning on the horn, and none of it moves faster than a crawl because there is simply nowhere for it to go.

At the center, on an overturned shipping crate that has been "his" throne for longer than most of the crowd has been alive, sits a boy who can't be older than sixteen, flanked by two lieutenants who look almost bored. He is not selling anything. He is watching hands — dozens of them, moving through pockets and bags all around you with the practiced economy of long apprenticeship — and every one of them answers to him. As you push into the crush, you feel at least one attempt on your person within the first ten seconds, testing whether you're worth the trouble.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**Jax Nine-Fingers** — the pickpocket king, impossibly young to hold the throne he holds, ruthless about tribute and oddly generous with information; runs the Crossing's cutpurses like a guild-clan of his own, deliberately mirroring Corran structure to earn legitimacy.

**Whisper** — Jax's chief lieutenant, an ex-Corran runner who quietly maintains actual guild-clan contacts Jax hasn't earned himself, using them to launder the crew's take.

**Inspector Voss** — a Sanctum "traffic actuary" who visits weekly to assess congestion-risk premiums on the lanes feeding the Crossing; despises Jax, can't touch him without cause, and is quietly building a file.

#### Security

Zone: Flats — the Crossing is Jax's court by universal, unspoken agreement; no faction has bothered contesting a five-way street knot this chaotic to hold.

Jax's cutpurses handle "security" by simply making trouble for anyone who threatens the flow of marks — a shove here, a tripped ankle there, escalating to a beating in a side-alley if someone won't take the hint. Actual weapons draw whistles and a scattering crowd; Jax's people melt away rather than fight armed opposition, then regroup and follow at a distance.

Sanctum's traffic-risk assessments occasionally trigger "safety sweeps" — brief, halfhearted patrols that everyone, including the patrol, treats as theater.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

Congestion-warning AR tags blanket the intersection, courtesy of Sanctum's actuarial monitoring — every visitor's HUD lights up with risk premiums and liability disclaimers nobody reads.

Jax's crew uses a private tag-language sprayed in AR graffiti only visible to those with the cipher — marking marks, marking heat, marking safe cut-through routes.

A hacker who taps the intersection's traffic-sensor mesh (nominally Meridian-owned) can watch the Crossing's entire crowd-flow in real time — useful for planning an ambush, an escape, or a very precise theft.

#### Plot Hooks

1. Voss's File. Inspector Voss offers real money for evidence tying Jax to a specific string of thefts — enough to finally justify a Sanctum crackdown. The crew has to decide whether cooperating buys them a powerful enemy in the Flats or a foothold with corporate risk-management.

2. The Mark Nobody Should Rob. One of Jax's cutpurses lifted a data-chip off a courier who turns out to be running for Ironclad's officer corps — war logistics, mid-siege. Jax wants the chip gone before Ironclad traces it back to the Crossing and turns the knot into a killing field.

3. Whisper's Real Ledger. Whisper has been skimming Jax's tribute to fund a genuine Corran guild-clan re-entry — buying her way back into a family that exiled her. If Jax finds out, the Crossing's fragile order collapses into a succession fight the crew gets caught in the middle of.

## Bowl Shrine

*Rice-and-blessing corner; Corran faith*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

The noise softens first, that's how you know you're close — the Bazaar's roar dropping to something almost hushed as the lane narrows and bends around a corner shrine wedged between a noodle-stall and a dead elevator shaft nobody's used in decades. Steam rises off a hundred bowls of rice and broth stacked on a long communal table, fogging the underside of the level above where it sags low enough here to string with paper lanterns instead of signage — the only stretch of the Shambles where corporate neon doesn't reach, replaced by warm, hand-strung light that makes the chemical rain dripping past the lantern-cords look almost gentle.

Even here the sky-traffic doesn't stop: a courier-drone banks around the shrine's lantern-line rather than through it, a small courtesy the whole district somehow honors, while below, ground-rigs detour half a lane out of their way rather than cut across the shrine's threshold-stones. The sound wall thins to something almost peaceful — a low chant in Corran dialect, the clatter of bowls, rain hissing on the noodle-stall's hot griddle — before the market's roar closes back in past the shrine's far edge like water closing over a stone dropped in a river.

A street-priest in patched grey robes looks up from ladling broth and meets your eyes with the particular patience of someone who has fed worse than you and expects to feed worse again.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**Brother Amsel** — keeper of the Bowl Shrine, a "Lamp on Ninth" street-priest who has fed this corner of the Shambles for fifteen years without asking a single question about where anyone's scrip comes from. Knows more secrets than any confessor because everyone talks over his broth.

**Little Wren** — a Corran orphan the shrine effectively raised, now grown into its fiercest defender and a formidable back-alley medic in her own right, using skills half-learned from a Caduceus grafter who owed Amsel a debt.

**Deacon Prine** — a rival street-preacher trying to fold the shrine's independent charity into a more centralized (and more Ferrum-friendly) faith network; Amsel resists, quietly, without ever raising his voice.

#### Security

Zone: Flats — treated as sacred ground by unspoken consensus; even hardened gang enforcers generally won't spill blood on the shrine's threshold-stones.

The shrine has no guards and needs none; its protection is entirely social — an attack here turns every faction that's ever eaten Amsel's broth against the attacker, from Corran guild-clans to Nyx runners who grew up on this corner.

The rare violation (a bounty hunter dragging a target out mid-meal, say) is remembered for years and answered eventually, patiently, by someone.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

Almost nothing. The shrine registers as dead air on the Wired — no ads, no SIN-readers, no AR tags at all, a genuine blank spot in the district's data-noise that locals call "the quiet corner."

That silence is itself valuable: dead-drops are sometimes left among the shrine's offering-bowls, exploiting the universal agreement that nobody surveils this ground.

A Technomancer passing through describes the spot as feeling different in the Wired itself — not blocked, just... unwatched, like the data-web politely looks away.

#### Plot Hooks

1. The Quiet Corner's Price. Deacon Prine's "centralizing" faith network is secretly a Ferrum front to map every soup-line and safehouse in the Flats for eventual demolition and redevelopment. Amsel needs proof before he loses his flock to a wolf in a Lamp's robes.

2. Wren's Patient. Little Wren is hiding a badly wounded deserter from the war climbing the foundations — an Ironclad soldier who saw something on the front he wasn't supposed to survive knowing. Healing him quietly is one thing; keeping him hidden from the people hunting him is another.

3. The Offering Nobody Claimed. Someone left an unmarked data-chip among the shrine's offering bowls, clearly meant as a dead-drop — but the intended recipient never came, and now three different factions are asking Amsel, very politely, if he's seen it.

## The Ear

*Nyx listening-post teahouse*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

You'd walk past it if you didn't know to look — a teahouse no wider than a single stall, its cyan sign flickering the character for "ear" in three scripts, tucked into a fold of the lane where two covered alleys nearly touch overhead and the underside of the level above presses down so close the steam from the teahouse's kettles seems to hold it up. Rain drums a slow rhythm on the patchwork roofing, running down in cyan-tinted ropes past a window fogged with condensation and cigarette smoke, and through it you can just make out a dozen small tables, each one occupied, each one very quiet.

Above the alley's narrow gap, a listening-drone hovers with its running lights dimmed to near-nothing, easy to miss unless you know Nyx's silhouettes, while below, foot traffic gives the teahouse's door a wide, unconscious berth — locals know this corner without being told what it is. The market's roar is still there, still a wall of six-tongued noise and drone-whine and hissing rain, but it feels muffled here, like the building itself is built to swallow sound rather than reflect it.

The old man at the counter doesn't look up as you enter, but somewhere in the back a comm-line clicks quietly onto record.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**"Uncle" Ghen** — the teahouse's public face, an unassuming elderly man who has run this counter for thirty years and is, in fact, one of Nyx's most trusted listening-post handlers in the Shambles; forgets nothing, repeats nothing without payment.

**Six-Cup Iyo** — a courier who moves recordings out of the Ear in the most mundane way possible — hand-delivered tea orders to specific addresses, the actual product hidden in the packaging.

**Analyst Kade** — a young Meridian data-runner who suspects the Ear is a Nyx listening-post and is quietly building a case, unaware that Ghen has known who he is since his second visit.

#### Security

Zone: Flats — Nyx-controlled, though the control is invisible; the teahouse projects total ordinariness and relies on that camouflage rather than muscle.

No visible security at all — that's the point. Trouble is handled by simply making a customer unwelcome (bad service, a "closed" sign, a quiet word) long before it escalates. If someone forces the issue, Ghen has exactly one phone call to make, and the people who answer it are not teahouse staff.

Discovery is the real threat here, not violence: if the Ear's true purpose becomes common knowledge, Nyx will burn the location and everyone in it rather than let it become a liability.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

The teahouse reads on the public Wired as exactly what it appears to be — a family business, decades of unremarkable transaction records, deliberately boring.

Underneath, Nyx runs a sophisticated passive-listening rig woven into the building's own utility lines, skimming conversations, comm-traffic, and Wired-chatter from half a block in every direction, filtered and forwarded through Ghen's "orders."

A skilled hacker who suspects the truth and digs past the boring surface finds an encrypted archive of years of recordings — a blackmail vault, a intelligence trove, or a death sentence, depending on whose secrets are in it.

#### Plot Hooks

1. Kade's Case. Analyst Kade has almost enough evidence to expose the Ear to Meridian corporate. Nyx wants the crew to either discredit him quietly or — more elegantly — recruit him before his bosses see what he's found.

2. The Recording That Shouldn't Exist. Six-Cup Iyo's latest delivery contains a recording of a Ferrum executive discussing troop movements for the war climbing the foundations — information Nyx wants to sell to the highest of several very interested bidders, and fast, before the source realizes it's compromised.

3. Ghen's Successor. Uncle Ghen is dying, slowly and quietly, and hasn't told Nyx he has no one to hand the listening-post to. He wants the crew to find someone trustworthy to inherit thirty years of secrets — or help him decide which secrets should die with him.

## Cage Fights

*Underground pit; Ironclad talent scouts*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

The bass hits you before the light does — a rhythmic crowd-roar pulsing up through a stairwell sunk below the market-lane's floor, steam and sweat-smell rolling up to meet the chemical rain drifting down through a jagged gap in the cover-plating above. Cage Fights isn't a building so much as a hole someone reinforced: a converted cistern three levels down, ringed with scavenged bleachers, its cage lit gold-white and merciless while the crowd above and around it disappears into shadow broken only by the glow of a hundred betting-slates.

Getting here means threading past the layered traffic clogging the lane above — a hovercab dropping off gamblers in sharp corp-adjacent clothes who clearly don't belong this deep, a cargo-crawler idling while its driver places a bet through the open door, courier-drones weaving overhead entirely uninterested in the spectacle below. Down in the pit itself the wall of sound is total: the crowd chanting, the announcer screaming over it in three languages, the wet crack of impact that quiets everyone for exactly one held breath before the roar comes back doubled.

Along the rail, unremarkable men and women in plain jackets watch the fighters with a very particular kind of attention — not gambling, evaluating.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**Bosun Kray** — the pit's owner-operator, an ex-fighter with a ruined voice and a good eye for talent; runs a clean house by underground standards — fixed fights get you banned, not just beaten.

**Talent Scout Marchetti** — an Ironclad recruiter working the rail, quietly evaluating fighters for augmentation contracts and combat-frame trials; offers a way out of the Flats that comes with a leash attached.

**Iron Rosa** — the pit's reigning champion, a Corran guild-clan fighter supporting her whole extended family on purse money, increasingly aware Marchetti wants to buy her contract outright.

#### Security

Zone: Flats — Kray's own house rules, backed by a handful of ex-fighters turned bouncers; no faction contests the pit directly, though Ironclad's recruiting presence buys them a quiet welcome.

Weapons are checked at the stair; the pit settles disputes in the cage, by design, which keeps most violence contained and refereed. Cheating (fixed fights, poisoned fighters) draws Kray's own brutal justice rather than any outside law.

Betting disputes occasionally spill into the crowd; Kray's bouncers break these up fast, more concerned with protecting the house's reputation than any individual gambler.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

The pit runs its own closed betting-network, invisible to the public Wired — odds, purses, and fighter stats all routed through Kray's private ledger to keep Sanctum's gambling-tax assessors from ever finding the operation.

Ironclad's recruiting arm quietly taps the betting-network anyway, tracking fighter performance data for talent evaluation without Kray's knowledge or consent.

A hacker tracing Marchetti's activity finds a recruitment pipeline feeding directly into war-effort combat-frame trials — fighters who "sign" rarely resurface in any records anyone can find.

#### Plot Hooks

1. Rosa's Contract. Marchetti has offered Iron Rosa a contract that would save her family from Flats poverty forever — and quietly ship her to the front of the war climbing the foundations as an augmented asset. She wants the crew to find out what actually happens to fighters who sign before she puts her name down.

2. The Fixed Fight. Someone poisoned an underdog to guarantee a betting result, and Kray's reputation — and possibly his life — depends on finding out who before the pit's backers decide he's lost control of his own house.

3. Marchetti's Real Ledger. The recruiter's talent pipeline is a cover for something darker: Ironclad is testing experimental combat-chrome on "volunteer" fighters and burying the failures. The crew stumbles onto a name that shouldn't be missing.

## Velvet Stair

*High-end simsense; invitation only*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

The Shambles doesn't stop being the Shambles here, but it dresses up for the occasion — a narrow, unmarked stair climbing off the main lane behind a curtain of real (real!) silk, guarded by a discreet velvet rope that somehow commands more respect than any gun in the district. Above the stairwell's mouth, the cover-plating has been patched with something that actually keeps the chemical rain out entirely, a small ostentatious luxury in a district where everyone else just lives wet, and the hush of it is its own kind of statement.

From the stair's landing you can look back down into the market's churn — the layered traffic of drones and hovercabs and grinding cargo-rigs, the forty stories of stacked signage bleeding pink and gold into standing rain, all of it suddenly feeling very far below, though you've climbed less than two flights. The Velvet Stair's own light is different: warmer, dimmer, more deliberate, filtering out from behind a second curtain where a host in an immaculate Obsidian-cut suit is already checking a list that, you suspect, does not have your name on it.

Whether that's a problem depends entirely on who sent you.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**Host Amaranth** — the Stair's imperturbable doorkeeper, technically Obsidian Velvet Room staff, in practice the final word on who ascends; reads intent better than any scanner and is rarely wrong.

**Patron Delvax** — a Spires-tier regular who slips down to the Flats specifically because the Velvet Stair offers experiences too dangerous or too honest for anywhere corporate eyes can reach; a mine of upper-level gossip for anyone who earns his trust.

**The Cartographer** — an anonymous simsense artist whose custom-built experiences (memories that never happened, cut with genuine skill) are the Stair's real draw; never seen, only heard through a vocoder, presence more legend than fact.

#### Security

Zone: Flats, but a genuine Velvet Room outpost — Obsidian's elite sub-corp holds this address directly, a rare pocket of real corporate authority sunk into the Shambles' chaos.

Security is invisible until it isn't: Amaranth's list controls entry, and anyone who forces the issue discovers the "unarmed" host and two "waiters" are extensively augmented Obsidian security, trained to end problems fast and quietly rather than make a scene.

Violence here triggers genuine corporate **Alert** — Velvet Room enforcers from higher in the tower, not street muscle, and they do not negotiate.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

A carefully curated blank: the public Wired shows an unremarkable tea import business, with the actual Velvet Room presence accessible only through an invitation-keyed private node.

Patron identities are scrubbed obsessively — even Obsidian's own systems anonymize who's inside, a rare instance of a corp protecting privacy rather than harvesting it, because their clientele demands it and pays for it.

A hacker skilled enough to breach the Stair's private node finds something unusual: fragments of the Cartographer's unreleased work, simsense experiences that read, unmistakably, like real memories laundered from somewhere they shouldn't have come from.

#### Plot Hooks

1. Delvax's Indiscretion. Patron Delvax let slip something he shouldn't have about Spires-level troop movement decisions in the war climbing the foundations — casually, to impress a companion who was listening far more carefully than he realized. Someone wants that companion identified before the leak reaches the wrong ears.

2. The Cartographer's Source. The crew is hired — anonymously, generously — to acquire "raw material" for the Cartographer's next piece: a specific memory, from a specific person, who is very much alive and does not consent to giving it up.

3. Amaranth's List. Someone has forged their way onto the Stair's guest list using a stolen SIN, and Amaranth wants it quietly resolved before Velvet Room corporate audits the breach and decides the whole location is compromised — taking Amaranth's career, and possibly the Stair itself, down with it.

## Grief House

*Unlicensed hospice; where runs go to die*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

There's no sign, no light worth mentioning, just a low doorway sunk between a shuttered stall and a support column too old to trust, marked only by a strip of red cloth tied to the frame — the Shambles' own quiet symbol for help is here, if you can still be helped. The rain finds this corner harder than most; the cover-plating above has rusted through in patches, and it drips steadily onto a doorstep worn smooth by feet that came here desperate and, often, feet that never left.

The market's noise doesn't stop at Grief House's door so much as it gets ignored — inside, past a curtain heavy with the smell of antiseptic and old blood, cots line both walls of a room lit by a single hanging lumen-strip, while somewhere above the roof-gap a courier-drone's running lights strobe faintly through the rust-holes, indifferent, and a ground-rig's engine-growl carries in from the lane like it's coming from very far away. Someone is crying quietly two cots down. Someone else isn't making any sound at all, and you can't tell yet if that's a good sign.

A woman with old blood on her sleeves looks up from a patient's side, already assessing whether you're here to be helped, to help, or to finish something.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**Doc Yuen** — Grief House's founder and only permanent staff, a Caduceus-trained medic who lost her license (or walked away from it — she tells the story differently each time) rather than turn away patients who couldn't pay or explain their wounds.

**Salt** — a recovering runner who stayed on as an orderly after Yuen saved his life; owes her everything and screens everyone who comes through the curtain accordingly.

**The Widow Cortez** — a grieving Ferrum-caste widow who funds Grief House anonymously, laundering guilt money from a husband's corporate sins into the only good she can find to do with it.

#### Security

Zone: Flats — utterly lawless by any formal measure, protected only by universal reluctance to bring violence to a dying room.

Yuen enforces exactly one rule with absolute authority: no weapons drawn inside, no matter who you're hunting or who's hunting you. Violators are physically ejected by Salt and whoever else is ambulatory, then barred forever.

The real danger at Grief House isn't violence — it's exposure. Patients here are frequently SIN-less runners hiding from Alert-level heat, and a corp sweep finding them here would be catastrophic for everyone inside.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

Deliberately, carefully dark. Grief House has no registered medical license, no SIN-reader, no AR presence at all — Yuen scrubs every trace she can, because a listed hospice is a hospice corps can subpoena records from.

The building draws just enough ambient Wired traffic to be forgettable — one more dead node in a district full of them.

A hacker looking specifically for it can find traces in encrypted Corran and Cinderhold networks — Grief House is passed along by word of mouth through channels the Wired's surface layer never touches, precisely because that's the point.

#### Plot Hooks

1. The Patient Who Knows Too Much. A dying runner in Yuen's care is the last living witness to a botched job that implicates a mid-tier Nyx lieutenant. He's fading fast, and at least two parties want to reach him before he does — one to silence him, one to hear his confession.

2. Cortez's Guilt. The Widow Cortez's dead husband was involved in a Ferrum demolition project that killed dozens of Flats residents; she wants the crew to quietly find the families and make restitution without her name ever surfacing, because if it does, Grief House's funding — and her own safety — disappears.

3. Salt's Old Crew. Salt's former running partners have tracked him to Grief House, not to kill him, but to drag him back for one last job tied to the war climbing the foundations — a job Yuen knows will finish what nearly killed him the first time.

## Scrip Alley

*Money-changers, forgers, chip-launderers*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

The alley announces itself in gold — dozens of hand-lettered exchange-rate boards glowing under cheap lumen-strips, each one a fraction of a percent different from its neighbor, and a crowd of quiet, intent people moving between them like traders on some ancient exchange floor shrunk down to fit a lane you could touch both walls of at once. Overhead, the cover-plating has been reinforced with scavenged plate-steel — someone here cares enough about their inventory to keep the chemical rain properly out — and what light gets through comes filtered gold and thin, pooling on countertops stacked with currency from a dozen jurisdictions and several that don't officially exist anymore.

The alley is too narrow for ground vehicles, which makes it a rare pocket of the Shambles where the crush is purely human — though above, in the gap between this level and the next, a courier-drone or two always seems to be loitering, and a hovercab's running lights sometimes flare past the alley's high, narrow window-slits, throwing long shadows across the money-counters below. The sound here is different from the rest of the market: quieter, more careful, the click of counting-machines and the murmur of exchange rates layered under the ever-present market roar and hissing rain, like a held breath in the middle of all that noise.

A changer behind the nearest counter doesn't look up, but her fingers never stop moving, and somehow you know she's already clocked exactly how much you're worth.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**Changer Voss** (no relation to Inspector Voss at the Crossing, a fact she finds endlessly, tiredly necessary to clarify) — the alley's most trusted currency-changer, runs a scrupulously honest exchange specifically because reputation is the only currency that matters more than nuyen down here.

**Ink** — a forger of documents and SIN-chips whose work is good enough to fool a casual Sanctum checkpoint scan; expensive, selective about clients, terrified of ending up someone's example.

**The Ledger Twins** — two Corran siblings who launder chip-based nuyen for a fee, running dirty scrip through a dozen legitimate-looking exchanges until it comes out clean on the other side.

#### Security

Zone: Flats — no single faction owns Scrip Alley; its order is enforced by the changers themselves, who collectively cannot afford a reputation for being robbable.

Every counter has a silent alarm and at least one armed partner out of sight; robbing one changer means facing the alley's entire community response within moments, since word travels counter to counter instantly.

Sanctum occasionally runs currency-fraud sweeps here; changers keep clean books for legitimate trades and entirely separate, unwritten arrangements for everything else.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

A genuinely wild mix: legitimate exchange-rate feeds broadcast openly for the honest trade, laid directly over encrypted private channels handling the alley's real business — laundering, forging, moving hot scrip.

SIN-reader density is unusually high, ironically, because legitimate currency exchange requires it — but Ink's forged chips are specifically built to satisfy those readers without triggering deeper Sanctum verification.

A skilled hacker tracing nuyen through the alley's legitimate-looking exchange records can, with effort, follow the Ledger Twins' laundering trail all the way back to its dirty source — a valuable and dangerous piece of financial archaeology.

#### Plot Hooks

1. Ink's Masterwork. Ink has been commissioned to forge a full SIN identity good enough to survive Spires-level scrutiny — a job so far above her usual work that she suspects, correctly, it's meant for someone deserting the war climbing the foundations under a new name.

2. The Ledger Twins' Mistake. The Twins accidentally laundered money belonging to a Nyx rival faction through a legitimate exchange, and now two separate parts of the same cartel are quietly hunting the same nuyen for different reasons.

3. Voss's Test. Changer Voss suspects one of her fellow changers has started robbing clients under cover of "bad exchange days" and wants the crew to catch them in the act — discreetly, because a public accusation without proof could tear the alley's fragile self-policing apart.

## Deadletter Post

*Anonymous message wall; job board*

### Player Description

*(read aloud as the crew arrives)*

You find it at the dead end of a lane that shouldn't be a dead end — a blank wall of salvaged plate-steel, three stories of it climbing up into the gap where the level above finally becomes visible as a ceiling instead of a rumor, and every centimeter of that wall is covered in paper. Actual paper — flyers, scrawled notes, printed bounty-sheets, pinned and layered and re-pinned for what looks like years, water-stained by the chemical rain that drips steadily down from a jagged seam directly above, as if even the tower itself can't resist adding commentary.

Above the dead-end's open gap, drones circle in a slow, endless loop — more of them than anywhere else in the Shambles, reading tags, cross-referencing faces, feeding some system nobody claims to run — while below, a boosted rig idles at the lane's mouth with its rigger watching the wall through the windshield rather than getting out, which tells you everything about who reads this board and doesn't want to be seen doing it. The noise here thins at last, funneled and swallowed by the dead-end's walls, leaving mostly rain-hiss and the shuffle-murmur of a dozen people standing at careful distances from each other, all reading, none making eye contact.

Somewhere on that wall, in ink or in AR-ghost or in a language you barely speak, there's a job with your name on it. You just have to find it before someone else does.

### Director's Section

#### Key NPCs

**The Postmaster** — nobody's real name for whoever maintains the wall's unspoken rules (no defacing others' postings, no burning a poster's anonymity); widely believed to be a rotating duty rather than one person, possibly a Cinderhold tradition gone citywide.

**Runner Beck** — a job-broker who works the wall professionally, translating cryptic postings into real contracts for a cut, effectively an unlicensed rival to Cassavir's Switchboard for those who can't get an audience uptower.

**Watcher Iyala** — a Meridian data-analyst who monitors the wall's postings for patterns, convinced (rightly) that Deadletter Post is an early-warning system for major shifts in the Flats' underworld — including troop movements tied to the war climbing the foundations.

#### Security

Zone: Flats — genuinely lawless, self-policed only by the community's investment in the wall's usefulness; nobody owns it, and everyone benefits from it staying neutral.

No guards, no gang colors claimed here — violence at the wall is rare specifically because it's considered bad practice; a poster torn down in anger or a reader mugged mid-read draws no organized response, but poisons the location's trust, which is worse for everyone long-term.

The real danger is informational, not physical: reading the wrong posting, or being seen reading it, can mark you as a target for whoever the posting concerns.

#### The Overlay (What the Wired Shows Here)

The wall has a ghost-layer: half its postings exist only in AR, tagged to the physical wall and visible only through basic public overlay — a genuinely democratic mix of paper for the SIN-less and AR-ghost for everyone else.

Surveillance-drone density here is the highest in the district, ostensibly Meridian's traffic-monitoring, but Watcher Iyala's pattern analysis suggests someone far more interested than Meridian's public charter would explain is watching this specific wall.

A skilled hacker can post — or remove — a Deadletter listing invisibly, making the wall a genuine, if contested, channel for anonymous job offers, warnings, and threats that never touch a corporate server.

#### Plot Hooks

1. Iyala's Pattern. Watcher Iyala has noticed a cluster of anonymous postings that, decoded, describe troop and materiel movements for the war climbing the foundations — someone is using the wall as an unwitting intelligence-relay, and she wants the crew to find out who before the wrong faction notices the pattern too.

2. Beck's Bad Deal. Runner Beck brokered a job off the wall that turned out to be a trap — the "client" was hunting the runner who answered, not hiring them. Beck wants the crew to find out who's using Deadletter Post to bait targets before it happens again and destroys the wall's usefulness for everyone.

3. The Postmaster's Successor. Whoever maintains the wall's unspoken rules has gone silent — no enforcement, no removals, just silence — and postings are starting to turn ugly: doxxing, threats, outright bounty-hunting disguised as job offers. Someone needs to find out what happened to the Postmaster, and whether the role can, or should, be filled again.
