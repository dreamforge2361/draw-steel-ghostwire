## The Default Home Hive

The campaign's default sprawl is Ossian Reach — the continent-anchored crown-jewel hive where the core campaign is assumed to run unless a table says otherwise. It is one of the great vertical hives of Veyra (Ch. 6): kilometers up into real sky, kilometers down into dead-level dark, built layer on ruined layer until no one alive remembers its ground floor.
- The name. Ossian is a founding-era name — the hive is raised on the fossilized bones of everything it was built over (os, bone; the hive is a reef of its own dead levels), and Reach is what the founders called the impossible thing they built: a structure that reaches from the Sinks to the Crown, from the buried dark toward the sunlit sky. Ossian Reach: the bone-tower that reaches for the light. Locals just say "the Reach," and to live in it is to spend your life somewhere on the climb between the two.
- Why it's the default. Ossian Reach is a HALO-dominant hive — fitting, since HALO Ascendant holds the sky, the power, and now the First Chair (Ch. 8). Its Crown is a HALO arcology-halo of orbital-fed light; every other conglomerate keeps a tower here because this is where the Congress convenes. That makes the Reach the political heart of Veyra — the one hive where all Twelve are present, the Sunlit Chair

sits, and Vera Solenne wages her campaign for it. A crew working the Reach is working the center of the board.
- The vertical, named in the Reach. The strata (Ch. 6) get local names here: the Halo (the Crown — HALO's orbital-lit apex), the Spires (upper corp towers), the Grid (the vast wage-body), the Flats (the semi-lawless underlevels where most runs begin), the Warrens (the written-off deep-underlevel warren-country beneath the Flats — the Reach's own name for the Low↔Squatter seam where corp maps end, salvage and off-grid holdouts live, and the descent toward the Sinks truly begins), and the Churn's country — the Sinks, the sunless dead-level dark where Nyx's moving Undermarket ("the Churn," Ch. 8) drifts and the buried past stirs. The way down is the campaign's descent-engine; the way out is the gate to the wastes (Ch. 6).
- What makes it runnable. Ossian Reach gives a table everything at arm's reach: all Twelve conglomerates present, the Congress and the First-Chair fight live overhead, Nyx's Undermarket as the crew's supply chain and job board in the Sinks, at least one known thin place (a Blacklight-tainted deep-net node under a Meridian tower, Ch. 8–9) and one corrupted zone bleeding up from a dead level, and a clear vertical spine — Halo to Sinks — that is the Lifestyle ladder made walkable.

### The three fixtures of the Reach

Every campaign hive needs three things a GM can grab on turn one: someone who hands out the work, somewhere the players should not go, and a way out of the box. Ossian Reach has all three, named and ready. The signature fixer — Mama Cassavir, “the Switchboard.” The Reach's default job-giver works out of a shuttered transit exchange on the upper Flats, a dead switching-station she's wired back to life and named herself after — an old woman (or so she appears) who sits at the junction of everything that moves through the middle of the hive. Soft chrome and a good cosmetician sell the grandmother; underneath she is a datajacked cyborg Controller with a Switchboard Deck and a private overlay-cell. Cassavir is the seam between up and down: she takes clean-branded contracts down from the Spires and corp cut-outs above, and dirty ones up from Nyx's Churn (Ch. 8) below, and she connects the crew to whichever end pays. She is not street muscle and not a mage — she is the phone, the one node everyone in the Flats routes through, and her power is that severing her would cut a hundred quiet deals at once, so no one does.
- Her code. Cassavir sells introductions, not people. She will burn a client to protect a crew's anonymity but never the reverse, because her whole franchise is being the fixer runners trust when every corp would sell them out — and she guards that reputation like the asset it is. Cross her once and you don't get killed; you get disconnected, which in the Reach is worse..), and a relationship to spend and endanger.

### The worst corrupted zone — Deadfall Nine

(“the Quiet Floor”). The Reach's known corrupted zone is a sealed dead level deep in the Sinks: officially Sublevel 9, a decommissioned Ferrum foundation-stratum (Ch. 8) that went dark a generation ago and was walled off rather than explained. Runners call it Deadfall

Nine, or just the Quiet Floor — because the defining symptom of the corruption here is the Unmaker's dead-air (Ch. 3): sound thins, then signal, then thought. Deep in the Nine the Wired doesn't reach, a scream carries no distance, and people who linger come back emptied — or don't come back at all. It is the closest surfaced wound of the Incursion in the whole hive, and it is climbing: the seal leaks, and the levels just above the Nine are slowly going quiet too.
- Why it's the worst. It is not monsters — it is un-making. The zone attacks the things a crew relies on to survive: comms, coordination, courage, memory. It's a place that punishes exactly the corporate certainty the Crown is built on (nothing is wrong) by being living, spreading proof that something is. Ferrum's Deepworks (Ch. 8) quietly maintains the wall and pretends the Nine is a structural condemnation; Nyx routes around it and sells the danger; HALO's feeds insist it isn't there. (Ch. 1, 3): the place “The Tithe” and “The Foundation Fault” point toward, the horror set-piece, the reason the Sinks are feared, and the ticking clock — every session it is a little larger. A crew that goes into the Nine and comes out changed is the campaign's coming-of-age. The wasteland settlement at the gates — Cinderhold. Where the Reach's lowest habitable levels open out onto the poisoned wastes of Veyra (Ch. 6) sits Cinderhold — a scrappy, half-free settlement clinging to the hive's outer gate, built from the Reach's cast-offs and the wasteland's salvage. It is the way out of the box and the closest thing to unowned ground the campaign has: too poor for the Twelve to bother annexing, too useful to destroy — the last stop before the wilds and the first market for anything the wastes drag in. Verdant Provision's Greenline Rangers (Ch. 8) use it as a forward post; Nyx runs contraband through it; wasteland caravans and hive-exiles meet here to trade.
- Who lives there. Cinderhold is the home of the cast-out: burned-SIN humans, Mutants exiled from the hive's clean levels (Ch. 5, Ch. 16), Changer packs and Goliar kinship-crews who'd rather be free and poor than owned and fed, and Revenants who frighten the tidy Crown. It is prejudiced, dangerous, and real in a way the sedated hive is not — the one place in the default setting where the underclass the Founding built (Ch. 1) governs itself, badly and freely. It is, quietly, the most hopeful place on the map: people who chose (Ch. 1) over people who were kept.. Cinderhold's protector — Ranger-Captain Cael Marrow, “the Gate.” Cinderhold has no mayor and no council that anyone obeys; what it has is Cael Marrow, a former Greenline Ranger field-captain (Ch. 8, Verdant) who worked this stretch of frontier for twenty years, buried too many crews to reclaimed ground that came back wrong (Ch. 3), and one day simply stopped filing his reports and stopped going back inside. Verdant lists him as lost on survey; the wastes know him as the reason Cinderhold is still standing. He went native in the truest sense — not gone feral, but gone loyal, to the cast-out settlement instead of the corp that trained him. He holds the gate with an ad-hoc militia of retired Rangers, Goliar kinship-crews (Ch. 19), and anyone who can hold a line, and he governs the only way the place can be governed: by being the one person hard enough, fair enough, and feared enough that the raiders, the cartels, and the corps all decide Cinderhold is more trouble to take than to leave alone.

- His authority. Marrow's power is not a title — it's that he is the settlement's living early-warning system.
He still reads the ground the way a Ranger does; he is the first to know when a caravan route has gone quiet, when the wastes are pushing something toward the gate, when the dead-air is spreading (Ch. 3). Cinderhold obeys him because the times it didn't, it got hurt. He keeps an uneasy back-channel to Verdant (old comrades who still slip him seed, water-tech, and ammunition off the books) and a colder one to Mama Cassavir up in the Flats (Ch. 8) — the two of them are the settlement's and the hive's twin switchboards, and they trust each other exactly as far as professionals do.
- The threat leaning on it — the Clean Route (Ch. 8, Kestrel Dynamics). Cinderhold's slow-motion existential threat is not raiders and not (yet) the Incursion — it is Kestrel Dynamics, whose autonomous-transport expansion (the “Clean Route,” Ch. 8) has finally noticed that the cheapest corridor from the hive into the deep wastes runs straight through the gate Cinderhold sits on. Kestrel doesn't want to conquer Cinderhold; it wants to civilize it — to buy it, brand it, insure it (a Sanctum partnership), pave its market into a logistics depot, and put the free settlement on the ledger at last. It comes not with an army but with contracts, clean water, and reliable pay — an offer that would end the raider threat overnight and end Cinderhold's freedom just as fast, splitting the settlement between those exhausted enough to take the deal and those who fled the hive precisely to never be owned again. Marrow is the fulcrum: the Falcon's people court him (a Ranger-captain is exactly the credible local face Kestrel needs), and Marrow knows the thing Kestrel doesn't — that the route it's so eager to pave runs through ground that is going quiet (Ch. 3). He is sitting on the same truth as the “Clean Route” run hook, from the other side of the gate. creeps closer, and the crew's own choices — do they help Kestrel prove the corridor safe, help Nyx keep it “dangerous,” or help Marrow keep the gate free — decide whether their home stays unowned. Marrow is the patron who gives wasteland work, the hard-but-fair authority who can bar a crew from the one safe settlement in the wastes, and, if the Incursion surfaces at the gate, the veteran who has seen it before and will finally have to say so out loud.

## What the Flats Are

Come up out of the transit tube and the first thing that hits you is the weight of the light. It does not fall from a sky — there is no sky, this low — it comes from everywhere at once: a hundred thousand signs stacked forty stories deep, noodle-stall holos and blessing-tier ads and simsense come-ons in six tongues, all of it bleeding pink and cyan and sodium-gold into the standing rain. The rain never really stops on the Flats. It falls from the condensation of a million air-scrubbers on the levels above, warm and faintly chemical, and it drags the neon down the girders in long smeared ribbons until the whole street looks like it is melting.

This is the fourth stratum down — **Lifestyle 4, Low** — the semi-lawless underlevel where the Machine's grip loosens just enough for a person to disappear into it. Above you, the **Grid** hums with wage-bodies who still have something to lose. Above them, the **Spires**; above them, the **Crown**, and the Halo burning at the top of the hive like a second sun so far away it reads as a rumor of morning that never actually arrives. Below you, the **Warrens** thin out into the written-off dark, and below that the **Sinks**, sunless and Signal-dead, where Nyx runs the Churn and worse things settle in the deep floors.

The Flats are the seam. Watched enough to be dangerous, dark enough to work in. This is the edgerunner's natural country — the place most runs begin, and the only place in the Reach where nobody chose the shape they were born to but everybody, at last, gets to choose.

## The Vertical & the Black Water

### Getting to & from the Flats — The Vertical Is the Obstacle

In the Reach, altitude is the class system, and movement is **vertical first, horizontal second**. You do not simply walk to the Flats or away from them — you ride the strata, and every boundary between them is a wall the Machine built to keep the wrong bodies on the wrong rungs. Understanding that wall is the first thing a crew learns and the last thing a run turns on.

**The two ways down, the two ways up.** There are only ever two kinds of passage between levels, and a crew's whole plan lives in the choice between them.

**The licensed spines — the corp way.** The great **transit spines** are the Machine's arteries: corp-run lift-stacks, maglev tubes, and cargo conveyors that move the wage-body up and down the tower on schedule. They are fast, clean, well-lit, and watched to the millimeter. Every one of them charges a fare on the bright ledger and, worse, every tier-boundary along the route is a **checkpoint**. To ride a spine is to trade time for exposure — you arrive quickly and you arrive seen.

**The grey transit — the runner's way.** Beneath and beside the spines runs the Flats' own circulatory system: **unlicensed lifts, smuggler's shafts, maintenance ways, and dead freight-conveyors** that fixers and runners use precisely because they do not scan you. Mama Cassavir knows every one of them, and access to the good ones is a favor, a fee, or a Renown check. Grey transit is slow, filthy, sometimes structurally unsound, and occasionally someone else's territory — but it is quiet, and quiet is the runner's native currency.

**Coming in.** Most crews first arrive on the Flats the way the book opens: up out of a dead transit tube into the weight of the neon and the warm chemical rain, or down a grey shaft from the Grid with the wrong SINs already burning in their pockets. Coming into the Flats is the easy direction — the Machine does not much care who sinks to the Low levels, only who tries to rise. A body drifting downward is a body giving up on the ledger, and the checkpoints going down are lighter for exactly that reason.

**Going up (inward — the hard climb).** This is where a run lives. To take a Flats crew — with visible chrome, borrowed identities, and gear that should not exist — up into the Grid, the Spires, or the Crown is to pass a rising wall of scrutiny, and the check gets harder with every rung:

**The SIN-scan.** Every boundary gate reads your System Identification Number. A blank simply will not transact — the gate won't open for a ghost. A crew moves up on **fake, burner, or spoofed SINs**, and a run can live or die on whether a borrowed identity survives one more scan. A **Wanted** flag on any crew member lights the scanner up like a flare.

**The posture-check.** Higher boundaries add a security-posture read on top of the SIN — dress, chrome, behavior, the company you keep. A Flats loadout reads wrong in a Spire lift, and the higher you climb the more the gate expects you to belong.

**Going down and out (outward — the gate to the wastes).** Below the Flats the passages stop being checkpoints and start being dangers. The **Warrens** and **Sinks** have no gates because they have no order — descending means the cameras die, the Signal thins, the maintenance stops, and local power (gangs, level-bosses, the Churn) fills the gap the corps abandoned. And at the very bottom of the map the hive opens outward at **the gate — Cinderhold**, the wasteland settlement where Ranger-Captain Cael Marrow holds the seam between the inner game and the wastes beyond. To leave the Reach entirely is to pass through Cinderhold onto the open ground, where the law is thinner, the Signal is thinner, and the war is in the open.

**Documents that will pass.** At least one clean **fake or burner SIN** per crew member (¥100–300 each, single-scan and disposable), matched to the tier they mean to reach. A SIN that reads Flats-Low will not open a Spire gate.

**A route, licensed or grey.** Decide the direction of exposure: buy fare and speed on the spine and plan to beat the checkpoints, or buy quiet on the grey transit and plan to survive the shaft. Cassavir sells routes as readily as she sells jobs.

**Chrome discipline.** Visible street chrome that reads proud on the Flats reads criminal in the Grid. Crews climbing high either run subtle 'ware or plan to talk (or hack) around the posture-check. What you wear is part of your cover.

**The specialist for the gate.** Know before you leave which door-opener you're leaning on — the Face for the talk, the Hacker for the spoof, the Scout for the bypass. A crew with no answer to a checkpoint is a crew that gets turned back at the first boundary.

**Gear for the vertical itself.** For the grey shafts and the outward climb, a **grapple-and-line or ascension rig** (~¥2,000) turns a lethal drop into a traversal. The vertical is terrain, and terrain kills the unprepared.

### The Black Water — The Cistern-Sea, the Flood-Cycle, and What Lives Below

Ask anyone in Slackwater where the water comes from and you'll get a shrug; ask where it goes and they'll lower their voice. The Flats sit on the drowned floor of the hive, and the black water that fills Slackwater's piers, the Drowned Market, and the culverts of the Spillway is not a river, not a sea, not a reservoir — it is all three and none of them, an engineered body of water the Reach has been quietly managing, and quietly lying about, for a thousand years.

**What the water actually is.** The Reach was built upward on the fossilized levels of everything it stood on, and the lowest of those old levels never drained — they drowned. What the docks call the **cistern-sea** is the flooded remains of the hive's own foundations: dead sub-levels, older ruins, and pre-Founding structure, filled to a black mirror whose bottom the charts stop pretending to know. It is not natural water. It is runoff, condensation, the endless warm chemical rain sheeting off every level's underbelly, and the slow weep of a thousand years of leaking infrastructure, all pooled in the dark and stirred by machines. It is warm in places it shouldn't be, and near the Sinkmouth it steams where something warmer wells up from below.

**Where it goes — down, not out.** Here is the thing the corps don't advertise: **the water does not flow out to the wastes. It flows down.** The Flats are the ground-level seam where the hive meets the poisoned wastes through the outer gate at Cinderhold — but the outer gate is a dry gate, a wall onto open ground, not a river mouth. The black water instead drains inward and downward, through sluices and culverts and the great sealed throat of the **Sinkmouth**, into the half-flooded dark of the **Warrens and the Sinks** below. Slackwater is not the bottom of the water; it is a landing on the way down. Everything that pools on the Flats is destined, eventually, for the deep dark — which is precisely why the deep dark is half-flooded, and precisely why the things stirring down there are so often described as rising through black water.

**Self-contained, cleaned, and rationed — the Ferrum machine.** The whole system is **corp-managed infrastructure, run by Ferrum Dynastic** through its dock authority and hydraulics crews. It is closed-loop by design: pumps lift and circulate, sluice gates route the flow, drains carry the overflow down, and reclamation plants skim the top layer clean enough to matter. But "clean" is a corporate word. The reclaimed water that reaches the Flats is expensive and rationed — a working freshwater still (like Dassa Wynn's in the Drowned Market) sells clean water at a price that's "either extortion or mercy depending who's asking," and the purest stills are quietly tapping unlicensed **Caduceus runoff lines** that aren't supposed to exist. The rain you can catch off a tarp is the only water most of the Flats can truly count on. Below the reclamation layer, the black water is not clean — it carries contaminants, disposed corp waste, and the occasional thing pulled up on a barge that makes people sick in ways a ripperdoc can't explain.

**The flood-cycle — the heartbeat under the heartbeat.** The single most important game-fact about the water is that **it moves on a schedule.** Ferrum's tidal system counts down to a **scheduled flood** — pumps and sluice gates cycling the water level up and down through the culverts and drains on a published timetable. When the sirens sound, the low channels fill; the Overflow basin empties in a controlled panic up its switchback ramps, the Spillway's smuggler-highways go from dry road to lethal torrent, and anyone slow, hurt, or fighting gets left to the water. The catch: **the official schedule lies.** Gangs like the Undertow keep their own flood-readers (some by data, some by feel, some half-mystical) because the published timetable is routinely altered — by Sanctum actuaries running "risk models," by Nyx to strand a rival's cargo, by whoever owns the sluice gate that day. A flood is a weapon, a clock, and a murder method all at once.

**Boats, barges, and the water lanes.** On the Flats, **the water is a highway.** Where the licensed lift-spines and maglev tubes move bodies up and down the tower, flat-bottomed **barges, skiffs, hover-launches,** **and cargo-hulks** move goods and people across the drowned district — and the water lanes are, by design, a smuggler's road. Nyx runs the wharves (the Long Pier), tolls the traffic (Tidewatch logs every hull that passes), and moves its Undermarket supply up from the Sinks by barge. Cinderhold's supply barges tie up at Marrow's Landing, the last legal mooring before the water gets serious. Real violence on the docks "comes by water, which most Edgerunners forget to watch." For a crew, a boat is often the quiet option a licensed checkpoint never is — and the reason Slackwater is where so many runs begin and end.

**The gate under the water — the Sinkmouth.** Yes, there is a gate under the inner sea, and it is one of the most charged locations on the Flats. The **Sinkmouth** is a channel wide enough for a barge where the black water doesn't stop at the seawall but goes down — sealed at its throat by a **three-story blast door**, Nyx-held, opened only on the Warden's authorization through mechanical locks deliberately kept **offline from the Wired** so nothing about it can be hacked from a distance. It is the water-road's version of a tier-boundary checkpoint: the last controlled passage between the Flats and the drowned Sinks below. A decker can't spoof it; a crew gets through it by going through the Warden (bribe, favor, information) — or by braving the black water around the seawall's edge, which has its own reasons to be avoided. Beyond the seal is understood to belong to no one, and nothing friendly.

## Gangs & Level-Bosses of the Reach

So this pass fleshes the street tier — the Grid and Flats gangs and level-bosses a new crew fights, works for, owes, and answers to — the immediate opposition and the first rung of Renown. Syndicates, cults, corp black-ops, and demon-touched cabals are sketched as the higher tiers a crew climbs toward. A crew earns its name against these people first. How street power works [frame] Below the Conglomerates (Ch. 8) and above the individual runner sits the hive's real day-to-day authority: whoever controls a level. Where the Grid's rented order thins into the Flats (Ch. 6), power localizes into two forms a crew must read:
- Gangs — collective territorial powers: a name, a turf, a racket, a color. They hold ground. A crew crosses them by working their level, and can ally, extort, join, or dismantle them.
- Level-bosses — individual powers who own a level or a district of one: the fixer, the crime-lord, the corrupt Grid-warden, the cult-mother. A gang answers to a boss; a boss answers to a corp, a syndicate, or no one. The level-boss is the crew's most common patron, obstacle, and betrayer all at once.
- The three currencies of the street: turf (who holds the ground), the lean (who owes whom protection, silence, or a cut), and Renown (what the street believes about you — the crew's real credit rating down here). Every faction below is defined by what it holds, who it leans on, and what it will trade.

The Grid gangs — order's underside The Grid is the wage-body's level: patrolled, licensed, watched (Ch. 21). Its gangs are therefore quiet, embedded, and deniable — they survive by not looking like gangs. They are the crime the panopticon tolerates because it's useful.
- The Metermen — the Grid's protection racket wearing a service uniform. They present as an unlicensed-but-tolerated "utility co-op" — they fix what the corp landlord won't, keep a corridor's lifts running, settle disputes — and they bleed every flat on the level for the privilege. Corp security lets them operate because a Metermen corridor is a calm corridor; the wage-workers pay because the alternative is nothing works. Holds: residential corridors, lift-stacks, the Grid's small commerce. Leans on: corp facilities-management (their quiet enablers). Trades: access, keys, a blind eye, the identity of who's behind on rent — a crew's easiest Grid contact and easiest Grid betrayal. Signature: a stamped brass "paid" chit left on a door.
- The Skinjobs — the Grid's chrome-and-BTL gang, dealers in financed cyberware's black-market twin:
bootleg 'ware, stolen skillwires, hot simsense, and the debt that comes with it (Ch. 21's financed-chrome culture, weaponized). They recruit the wage-worker who wanted the augment they couldn't afford, and own him through it. Holds: the ripperdoc trade below Caduceus's licensed line, the Grid's vice. Leans on: a Caduceus mid-manager skimming inventory. Trades: cheap chrome, a fast install, the name of who's running hot 'ware — and a hook in your spine. Signature: mismatched glowing optics worn on purpose.
- The Nightshift — not quite a gang: the loose brotherhood of Grid workers who see everything on the levels the corp forgets to watch after hours — janitorial, maintenance, mortuary, night-transit. Information more than muscle. A crew that treats them as people (not tools) gains the Grid's best eyes; a crew that doesn't finds every door locked. Holds: the after-hours Grid, the maintenance ways (the grey transit of Ch. 21). Leans on: nobody — which is their power and their peril. Trades: what they saw, which way the drones swept, which conduit is dead. The Flats gangs — run-country The Flats are the edgerunner's natural country: dim, dense, semi-lawless, where corp authority thins and the cameras go to packs (Ch. 6/21). Its gangs are loud, territorial, and real — they don't hide, they hold. This is where most runs begin and most street beefs live.
- The Ninth Ward Kings ("the Kings") — the Flats' dominant turf gang, a broad street-nation more than a crew: hundreds of members, a dozen sub-sets, colors on every wall of the levels they claim. They are the law where corp law isn't — they tax the markets, run the protection, settle the blood-debts, and keep a brutal, real order that the residents half-hate and half-depend on. Not evil, exactly; sovereign. A crew working their turf works with their permission or against their whole nation. Holds: the central Flats markets and residential blocks. Leans on: Mama Cassavir (Ch. 8) brokers their peace with the fixer-world; answers to no corp, which makes them a problem the Conglomerates would love to solve. Trades: safe passage, muscle, turf-knowledge, a war if you cross them. Signature: a nine-pointed

crown tag.
- The Rust Saints — a Goliar (Ch. 12) kinship-crew turned gang: heavy-caste laborers cast out of the industrial levels who kept their kinship-loyalty and their strength and now run the Flats' hardest muscle, salvage, and demolition rackets. Plain-spoken, fiercely loyal inward, terrifying outward; they honor deals to the letter and answer betrayal with collapse (literally — they know how to bring a level down). Half of them keep a rough, real street-faith (Ch. 19) that the Rust Saints name is only half-ironic. Holds: the industrial-edge Flats, salvage fields, the demolition and heavy-hauling trade. Leans on: kinship, not corps. Trades: breach-muscle, salvage, safe demolition, absolute reliability — the crew's best hired heavy and worst enemy. Signature: a rusted iron nail worn as a pendant.
- The Glass Vipers — the Flats' fast, vicious up-and-comers: a young simsense-and-speed gang running
BTL, stolen drones, and the grey-transit smuggling shafts (Ch. 21), expanding by taking what older gangs got comfortable holding. Reckless, wired, media-savvy — they film their runs. They're the gang most likely to be a rival runner crew's backers, and the one most likely to start a war they can't finish. Holds: the grey-transit shafts, the Flats' edges and contested blocks. Leans on: the Glass Vipers court whoever's rising — lately, quiet Nyx (Ch. 8) money. Trades: speed, smuggling routes, drones, and trouble. Signature: a green-glass shard; run footage posted to the feeds.
- The Hollow Men — the Flats' dread: a corruption-touched gang from the Flats/Sinks boundary (Ch. 3/5)
whose members have gone thin — too much bad chrome, bad BTL, bad ground, or worse. Less a gang than a symptom; the other gangs give their blocks a wide berth. A crew that tangles with them has stopped doing a street run and started touching the war (Ch. 3). Holds: the dead blocks toward the Sinks, the abandoned levels. Leans on: nothing living. Trades: they don't. This is the seam where the street tier touches the cosmic one. The level-bosses — who owns the ground Above the gangs sit the individuals who own levels. A starting crew's whole early career runs through people like these — patrons who hire them, powers who own the turf they work, and the ones they'll eventually have to choose to serve or break.
- Mama Cassavir, "the Switchboard" (Flats) [established — Ch. 8] — the crew's canonical entry fixer and the closest thing the Flats have to a neutral power: she doesn't hold turf, she holds connections, and every gang above needs her more than she needs any of them. Patron, broker, and the person who keeps the Flats' peace by knowing everyone's leverage. The default first level-boss a crew works for.

## Sister Hive — Ashenreach

Ossian Reach remains the **default home hive** for play. Ashenreach exists so Directors can stage rival-city politics, long caravan runs, and "someone else's Grid" without inventing a nameless glow on the horizon.

---

### Overview

**Ashenreach** is the inland sister-hive: dryer, higher, and harder-edged than coastal Ossian Reach. Where the Reach climbs out of drowned floors and black water, Ashenreach climbs out of ash basins and old burn-scars — stacked megablocks braced into mountain shoulders, neon cutting pink and cyan through dust haze instead of sea fog. Locals call the lit core **the Ember Sprawl**; outside the walls the wastes read as charcoal and glass, not swamp.

Conglomerate presence skews **Ferrum / Ironclad / Meridian** in the popular story (bones, enforcement, traffic-net), but Ashenreach is not a single-corp company town. It is a full Hive: Halo and Spires overhead, a colder Grid, underlevels that choke on ash instead of floodwater. Signal light hits the upper city hard — some runners swear the halo sits closer here, or that the Wired speaks with a thinner, more brittle voice once you leave Reach Trace routes.

---

### Rivalry with Ossian Reach

The two Hives need each other and hate the bill.

- **Trade & caravans.** Hardened lines and scrap roads cross ~300 miles of jungle, desert, ruins, and hills. Ashenreach wants Reach wet-dock freight and underlevel black markets; Ossian Reach wants Ashenreach's dry-route ore, Ferrum plate, and "clean" Meridian routing that does not drown every wet season.
- **Jurisdiction & Trace.** Neither Hive's Trace Alert fully owns the wastes. Border cold-sites, relay masts, and dead zones become proxy wars: who bills the ICE, who eats the biofeedback when a caravan goes dark.
- **Pride & propaganda.** Reach crews call Ashenreachers **Ashers** or **Ember-born** — stiff, sky-scared, too fond of walls. Ashenreach crews call Reach folk **Drowners** or **Flats-rats** — soft from black water, addicted to neon and noise. Both are half true.
- **Corp chess.** Conglomerates play the Hives against each other: undercut a Reach Sanctum contract by routing through Ashenreach; starve an Ashenreach district by throttling Marrow Gate hours at Cinderhold. Runners get hired as the deniable middle.

Cinderhold and the Outer Gate sit on Ossian Reach's wasteland seam, not inside Ashenreach — but every serious Ashenreach caravan still smells of Cinderhold smoke before it hits the Ember Sprawl.
