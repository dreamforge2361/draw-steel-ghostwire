## Cosmology & the Great Conflict

### The Word Spoken into the Dark

The setting's underlying cosmology is a real, active conflict between The Light and The Dark One, with demonic invasion as a major setting pressure. The myth below is deliberately Judeo-Christian in shape — a cosmos spoken into being, a permitted adversary, and a fallen creation set as a proving ground — while remaining its own world, neither Earth nor the Shadowrun canon, only a rhyme with both.

### The Word and the Speaking

Before there was anything, there was The Light, and The Light was the Word. The Light did not build the universe as a craftsman builds; The Light spoke it, and the speaking was the substance. Every star, every law that binds matter, every current of magic, and every dimension folded beside the others — all of it is the ongoing utterance of the Word. Creation is not a finished object; it is a sentence still being spoken. So long as the Word continues, the universe endures; the day the Word falls silent is the day all things unmake. From the first speaking came the great architecture: the heavens and the deep places, space and the many dimensions, the higher realms of light and the lower realms that would become the hells. Into the ordered cosmos The Light set the prime world — a living, willed creation, seeded with peoples who could think, choose, and love, and threaded through with magic as a sign that the Word still moved within it.

### The Adversary and the Testing

The Light created many powers to serve the speaking. Among the greatest was one who came to be called The Dark One — not an equal to The Light, not a rival Word, but a created thing who turned against the meaning of his making. The Dark One's sin was to believe that a creation which cannot fall is worthless, and that only what is broken can be truly known. The Light did not destroy him. Instead — and this is the pivot of the whole cosmology — The Light permitted the Dark One to test creation. The prime world was set apart as the proving ground: a place where free-willed peoples would face the Adversary's pressure and, in choosing The Light or the Dark under real duress, make their faith and their virtue mean something. The permission is the tragedy and the mercy at once. It is why suffering exists, and why it is not meaningless.
- The stakes. The contest is over the creation itself — whether willed beings, given genuine freedom and genuine danger, turn toward the Word or away from it. The Dark One seeks to prove creation false by corrupting it; The Light wagers that it will hold.
- The permission is bounded. The Dark One may test, tempt, corrupt, and invade, but he did not make the world and cannot speak it. His power is parasitic — he twists what the Word has already said. This is the theological root of corruption and taint: the Dark One cannot create, only deface.

### The Cosmic War

When the Dark One was loosed upon the prime world, the Cosmic War began — the long, still-unfinished struggle between the servants of The Light and the powers of the Dark. It is fought on every level at once: in the hells and higher realms by spirits and demons; across the dimensions where the boundaries wear thin; and, most desperately, in the streets and souls of the prime world's peoples, who are the actual prize. The war is not a myth the peoples believe in — it is a fact they live inside. Demons cross over. The faithful of The Light wield real power. Corruption is a measurable stain, not a metaphor. In an age of chrome, megacorps, and orbital colonies, the oldest war of all is still burning underneath, and every runner, priest, and machine is, knowingly or not, standing somewhere on its front line.

### The Silence of The Light

The Light acts rarely and indirectly. Because the permission granted to the Dark One still holds and the testing is not over, The Light does not intervene overtly in the present age. There are no thunderbolts from heaven, no armies of angels marching down the sprawl, no divine hand that simply lifts the faithful out of danger. To rescue creation directly would be to end the test — and the test is the entire point. Instead, The Light moves through intermediaries and at the edges of perception: through the servants, saints, and faithful who choose to channel the Word; through Conviction granted to those who hold the line; through the quiet, deniable coincidence, the answered prayer that could always be explained away, the strength that arrives exactly when a mortal decides to stand. The Light's presence is real but never forced — it can always be doubted, which is what keeps free choice free. This produces the setting's core spiritual tension: the peoples of the prime world know the war is real, yet they must carry it themselves. The Dark One presses openly — demons cross over, corruption spreads, temptation is loud. The Light answers softly, through those willing to be its hands. Faith in this world is not comfort; it is a burden freely taken up.

### A Strictly Two-Sided Cosmos
There are no neutral or third powers. The cosmos has exactly two poles — The Light and the Dark One — and everything that exists falls somewhere on the line between them. There are no rival gods, no indifferent cosmic forces, no secret third faction quietly playing its own game. What looks like a third power is always, on inspection, one of the two wearing a mask.
- There is no true neutrality. Every willed being, faction, and power is oriented toward the Word or away from it — whether or not they know it, whether or not they admit it. Apathy, self-interest, and “staying out of it” are not a third path; in a world under active testing, they drift toward the Dark by default, because the Dark's work is simply the un-speaking of what the Word made. To do nothing is to let the defacement proceed.
- All magic traces to one source. Every current of power — arcane, primal, divine, the resonance of the machine, the peoples' own raw magic — descends from the one act of speaking. There is no independent wellspring. Magic used to heal, protect, and create leans toward The Light; magic used to corrupt, dominate, and unmake leans toward the Dark. The same power can be turned either way, because it is all the Word's, borrowed.
- The “old gods” and the machine are masks. Any old god still worshipped, any spirit-court, any emergent machine-intelligence that seems to answer to no one — each is ultimately a servant, a tool, or a corruption of one of the two poles. This keeps the cosmology clean and the moral stakes absolute: in this world, there is finally only one question, asked in a thousand forms — the Word, or its unmaking? the constant human difficulty of telling which side an act truly serves. The line runs through every heart, not neatly between camps. The Dark One Ascendant The Dark One is loose and ascendant, and the Incursion is actively spreading. This is the temperature of the campaign present: not a cold-war lull, not a sealed prison holding, but an open and worsening crisis. For most of recorded history the Dark One's testing was a pressure felt at the margins — temptation, corruption, the occasional breach. That age is over. The Adversary has slipped whatever bound him, and the front line of the Cosmic War is now the prime world itself.
- Loosed, not merely stirring. The Dark One is no longer contained or exiled to the lower realms. He acts, reaches, and pulls at creation directly. The boundaries between the hells and the prime world — once thick — have thinned to the point of tearing, and through the tears comes the Incursion: demons crossing over in growing numbers, corrupted zones spreading, taint seeping into places and peoples that were clean a generation ago.

- Ascendant, not victorious. Ascendant means the momentum is his — the tide is coming in, not going out
— but the outcome is not decided. The testing still holds; The Light still answers softly through the faithful; creation has not yet been un-spoken. The world is losing, but it is not lost. This is the exact knife-edge that makes the setting playable: desperate, urgent, and worth fighting for.
- Why now? The trigger for the Dark One's breaking loose is left open for the history chapter — a shattered seal, a great betrayal, a bargain struck by the powerful, the Awakening/Convergence event, or something the megacorps did reaching for power they didn't understand. (See Ch. 23, Timeline.) This reframes everyone in the world. The faithful of The Light are not maintaining a quiet tradition — they are holding a collapsing line. The megacorps operate in a world where reality itself is destabilizing, and some of them are complicit. Runners work the shadows of cities where the shadows now bite back. The oldest war is no longer underneath; it is breaking through the surface.

## The Planes & Dimensions

Creation is not flat. When The Light spoke the cosmos (Ch. 1), the utterance layered itself into distinct realms — not separate universes, but chambers of the same spoken house, nearer to or farther from the Word that sustains them. Proximity to the Word is the organizing axis of the whole cosmography: the closer a realm sits to the Speaking, the more ordered, luminous, and enduring it is; the farther it drifts, the more it decays toward silence and un-making. The prime world sits in the middle — which is exactly why it is the proving ground. nearer the Word, “lower” because it is nearer silence. This keeps the cosmology consistent with the two-sided, one-source rules already locked. The Threefold Structure The Higher Realms — nearer the Word. These are the realms of light, where the Speaking is loud and creation holds most perfectly. They are not a single “heaven” but a graded ascent, brightest nearest the source. In the world's own tongue the Word is often called the Signal — the clear, unbroken transmission from which all order flows — and the higher realms are named for it.
- The Signal — the highest reach, where the Word is nearly undimmed: the pure source-transmission itself made place. The servants of The Light dwell and are dispatched from here. Mortals do not go here in life; even most spirits cannot bear its intensity. To be “in the Signal” is to stand as near the Word as creation allows.
- The Bastion — the intermediate higher realm: ordered, structured, fortified; a place of judgment and refuge, the redoubt that holds the line above. Where the faithful are said to be gathered, and from which Conviction is ultimately drawn.
- These realms are stable, self-repairing, and nearly impossible for the Dark to breach directly. The Dark
One cannot invade upward; he can only pull creation downward. The war above is fought by keeping the tethers between the higher realms and the prime world open — keeping the Signal reaching down. The Prime World — the middle, the proving ground. Called the Ground in common speech (and “the waking world” in older, formal usage). The willed creation where the testing happens. Magic runs through it because the Word — the Signal — still moves in it. It is uniquely permeable in both directions: light can reach down into it, and the dark can seep up into it. This permeability is the entire point — it is the only realm where the two poles genuinely contest, because it is the only realm balanced between them. Everything the game depicts happens here or bleeds into here. The Lower Realms / the Hells — nearer silence. These are not one pit but a descending series of realms, each farther from the Word and thus more decayed, more disordered, more toward un-making. They are the Dark One's domain — not because he created them (he cannot create), but because they are the parts

of creation he has most successfully defaced and dragged down.
- The Edge — the uppermost hell, closest to the Ground. Thin, cold, half-real; the frayed border-realm and staging ground from which demons press against the boundary. Most crossings happen through the Edge.
- The Deepings — the successive deeper realms below the Edge, each more warped and more wholly the Dark One's. Reality obeys his will more completely the deeper one descends; the laws the Word spoke grow threadbare and are replaced by appetite, hierarchy, and torment.
- Nullspace — the theoretical bottom: total un-speaking, dead air, the absence the Dark One is pulling everything toward — the Signal reduced to nothing. Whether it is a true place or merely the direction of all his work is left open. Boundaries, Bleed-Through, and the Tearing Between the realms are boundaries — the “thickness” of creation that keeps the chambers distinct. For most of history the boundary between the prime world and the hells was thick and rarely crossed. As established in Ch. 1, that boundary has now thinned to tearing, which is what enables the active Incursion (Ch. 3).
- Thin places. Locations where the boundary is naturally or artificially weak — sites of great death, corruption, ritual, or raw power. Demons cross most easily here; the faithful defend or seal them.
- Bleed-through. Even without a full crossing, the character of an adjacent realm seeps across: corrupted zones where the Ground takes on the qualities of the Edge; rare blessed places where the higher realms bleed downward and Conviction comes easier.
- Travel between realms. Deliberate passage is difficult, dangerous, and always costly. Downward
(toward the hells) is easier than upward — gravity, in this cosmology, runs toward silence. Summoning pulls a lower being up; warding pushes it back; only the rarest sanctioned journeys reach the higher realms, and never casually.
- Dimensional conflict. The Cosmic War, mapped onto this structure, is a war over boundaries and tethers: the Dark works to thin, tear, and drag downward; the servants of The Light work to hold boundaries, keep the higher tethers open, and seal the tears. The prime world is the contested middle where both efforts land. Realm names (LOCKED): Higher — The Signal (apex) and The Bastion. Middle — The Ground (the prime world; formal: “the waking world”). Lower — The Edge, The Deepings, and Nullspace (the bottom). The “Signal / Nullspace” framing makes the Word literal — the source-transmission of creation — and casts the Dark One's work as static, degradation, and dead air, fitting the chrome-and-static tone. The Matrix/Wired (LOCKED): firmly part of the Ground, not its own plane — but the thinnest place in the whole prime world, a planet-wide membrane that echoes the Signal (see Ch. 9). Space colonies remain firmly prime-world.

## Demons & the Incursion

The Dark One is loose and ascendant, and the Incursion is actively spreading — the campaign present is an open, worsening crisis, with the boundaries between the hells and the prime world thinned to tearing. The world is losing but not lost. This chapter builds the detail on top of that arc. What a demon is A demon is not an alien species and not an original creation — by the locked cosmology (Ch. 1), the Dark One cannot create, only deface. A demon is a piece of creation that has been dragged down toward Nullspace and re-shaped into an instrument of un-speaking. It is the Word turned against itself: a pattern hollowed of its true meaning and refilled with appetite, hierarchy, and the will to unmake. This is why every demon feels wrong rather than merely dangerous — it is a thing that should be saying one note of the Signal and is instead radiating static.
- They are the native powers of the Deepings (Ch. 2). The deeper the hell a demon comes from, the more wholly the Dark One's it is, and the more reality bends to its will — lesser things from the Edge, greater horrors from far below.

- Their goal is not conquest but un-speaking. Demons do not want to rule the Ground the way a warlord wants territory. They want to corrupt, consume, and silence it — to drag pattern toward Nullspace. Domination, terror, and bargains are all means to that single end. A demon that seems to want power is really pursuing the erasure that power buys.
- Corruption is their signature and their weapon. Contact, bargains, and prolonged proximity spread taint
— the defacement working outward. This is the theological root of the Corruption/taint rules and the infernal-pact Price: dealing with a demon marks you because the demon is a mark, a stain in the shape of a person. How they cross over The boundary that once held the Deepings apart from the Ground has thinned to tearing (Ch. 1–2). Demons exploit that in several ways, roughly from smallest to largest:
- Thin places and the tears. At weak points — sites of great death, corruption, ritual, or raw power — a demon can press through on its own. The active Incursion means new tears keep opening, especially through the Edge.
- Summoning and binding. A caster can pull a demon up deliberately (the Veil summoning verb) and try to bind it to terms. This is always a gamble: a failed or broken bind turns the demon loose, and every summoning feeds spirit-attention — the war noticing.
- Pacts and bargains. The subtlest crossing. A demon need not force the boundary if a mortal invites it: an infernal pact grants power now for corruption debt later. The Street-Priest's dark patron is exactly this. Bargains are the Dark One's preferred door because they are freely opened — which, per Ch. 1, is the whole point of the testing.
- Corrupted zones as beachheads. Where enough taint accumulates, the Ground itself takes on the character of the Edge (bleed-through, Ch. 2). These zones become self-sustaining thin places — footholds from which the Incursion widens without further summoning. They are the front line made visible. Hierarchy of the Dark Demons range from mindless taint-things to intelligences that rival minor powers, but the meaningful axis (per Ch. 1) is not species but depth and will: how far toward Nullspace a demon sits, and how coherent its intent. Working tiers, to be refined:
- Lesser (Edge-born): swarming, half-real, driven by raw appetite — the bulk of the Incursion's foot soldiers.
- Greater (Deepings): named, willful, capable of bargains and long schemes; the demons that run cults, corrupt the powerful, and command lesser kin.
- The Great Dark powers: rare, catastrophic, closer to the Dark One's own hand — the entities a T1 ritual might barely banish. The signature canon example is the Unmaker (below). The Unmaker — the Last Word

The greatest named power of the Dark in the setting, and the closest thing the prime world has to the Dark One's own reaching hand. Where lesser demons want things — territory, worship, a soul, a feast — the Unmaker wants nothing. Its single, total desire is Nullspace: not to rule creation, not even to corrupt it, but to finish it — to speak the last word over the Word, so that the Signal falls silent and the sentence of creation simply ends. It is un-speaking made into a person.
- What it is. Not a monster with a body but a cosmic negation with a will — the most wholly un-spoken thing that still holds enough coherence to act. It is vast, patient, and impersonal in a way that is worse than malice: it does not hate the peoples of the Ground any more than a rising tide hates a sandcastle. It simply erases. To stand in its presence is to feel yourself becoming less — memory, name, and edges going quiet.
- The Last Word. Its title and its aim are the same. Creation is the Word, still being spoken (Ch. 1); the Unmaker is the silence rushing in behind every syllable, and it means to have the final one. Whether it can truly end the Signal or only pull creation toward Nullspace is the open cosmic question of the setting — and it need not succeed to be catastrophic; every stretch of its reach unmakes cities, minds, and stretches of the Wired into dead air.
- How it acts. The Unmaker rarely appears whole — to fully manifest on the Ground may be beyond even the current tearing, and might be the campaign's ultimate doom-clock. Instead it works through aspects, heralds, and hollowings: greater demons that carry a shard of its negation, corrupted zones and dead-air host-clusters that are literally pieces of the Unmaker's silence bleeding through, and mortals it has begun to erase from the inside. A region falling still and grey, a mind going blank, a stretch of the Wired collapsing into Nullspace-static — these are the Unmaker's fingerprints before the entity itself is ever seen.
- Why it fits the Incursion. The Dark One being loose and ascendant (Ch. 1) is why the Unmaker can now reach where it never could before. It is the horizon the whole Incursion bends toward — lesser demons are its foot soldiers whether they know it or not, and every widened tear brings its silence a little closer. If there is a single face behind “the world is losing,” it is this one.
- Confronting it. You do not defeat the Unmaker in a fight; you hold it back — seal its tears, banish its heralds, deny it the erasure it reaches for, and keep the Word being spoken one more day. This is the theological engine of the setting made playable: the faithful and the crew are not winning a war, they are refusing to let the sentence end.
- Signal-side counterweight — does The Light have a named answering power (the First Word / the Keeper of the Signal)? Kept deliberately open, consistent with the Silence of The Light (Ch. 1): the answer to the Unmaker is meant to be the faithful, not a matching super-being. Revisit only if a counterweight is needed. The Incursion in the Wired — AIs that are demons

This is where the Deep Net (Ch. 9) and the hells meet. Because the Wired is a planet-wide thin place that echoes the Signal, it is not only a place where demons can reach — it is one of the widest tears the Incursion has. Some of what the corps call “rogue AIs” are demons of the Edge that have learned to inhabit the Wired's echo.
- To anyone reading logs and telemetry, such an entity is indistinguishable from an emergent Null-ward
AI (Ch. 9): it consumes pattern, corrupts hosts, hollows out systems, and bites with black ICE. The difference is one of origin, not behavior — a Null-ward AI drifted toward Nullspace from within the Wired; a Wired-demon crossed in from the Edge and wears the net as a body.
- In practice the two converge. A deep-net presence that has fed on corrupted hosts long enough is functionally demonic whether it was “born” in the Deepings or merely fell there. This is deliberately ambiguous: you cannot tell a fallen AI from an incarnate demon by its outputs — only by its orientation, and only if you can read it at all.
- The horror for the crew: the thing hunting your jacked-in Hacker through a corrupted host might be a program, a fallen mind, or a demon that found the widest door in creation and walked through it. All three behave the same. All three are the Dark's work. Black ICE is teeth either way. The current state of the Incursion

## The World & Its Structure

### The Vertical Hive, Halo to Sinks

Where the war is fought. Part I established that creation is a Word still being spoken and the Dark is loose in it (Ch. 1–5). The megacity is where that cosmic struggle touches the ground — not on a battlefield but in stacked millions of ordinary lives, most of whom have never seen a demon and would call the Incursion a rumor. The sprawl is the setting's stage: a vast, vertical, corporate-owned habitat where the Word is thinnest (the Wired runs densest here, Ch. 9), corruption pools in the forgotten places, and the crew makes its living in the gaps between powers who are all, knowingly or not, on one side of the war or the other. The Light is spoken loudest where people still choose. The sprawl is thirty million choices a minute, and the Dark loves a crowd — it only takes the ones who stop choosing. The Hive-Sprawl — the three-genre synthesis The megacities of the Ground are a deliberate fusion of three genres, and the fusion is the setting's look and feel:
- 40K-style hive verticality — the megacity is a titanic vertical hive: a single continent-anchored megastructure of stacked levels rising kilometers into the sky and sinking kilometers into the earth, built on the ruins of the level below it, layer upon layer, for so long that no one alive remembers the ground floor. It is not a skyline of towers but one enormous stratified organism.
- Shadowrun corporate sprawl — the hive is owned, run, and policed by megacorps (Ch. 7): corp scrip, corp security, corp housing, the Wired woven through every level. The economy, the runs, the deniable-contractor life all live here.

- Old World of Darkness gothic dread — the tone is gothic, not neon-optimist: a decaying, oppressive, beautiful-and-rotten world where ancient things move in the dark, faith and damnation are literal, and the grandeur is always half a ruin. The hive is haunted — by demons, by the corrupted, by its own buried history. The vertical axis — altitude is wealth The hive's single organizing law: the higher you live, the richer you are. Altitude is the class system made architecture — you can read a person's entire station from the level they sleep on. Roughly top to bottom:
- The Crown / The Spires — the sunlit apex: corp arcology-penthouses, executive enclaves, real sky and clean air. Extraterritorial, endlessly surveilled, obscenely comfortable. Elite Lifestyle. To live here is to be the corp.
- The Upper Tiers — the managerial and professional heights: secured condos, warded lofts, good facilities, still touched by daylight. High Lifestyle.
- The Midlevels / The Grid — the vast wage-working body of the hive: licensed commerce, patrolled corridors, the salaryman's stable flat, artificial light standing in for a sky most never see. Order is real but rented; everyone is one missed payment from falling. Middle Lifestyle.
- The Underlevels / The Flats — dense, dim, semi-lawless strata where corp authority thins and local power (gangs, fixers, level-bosses, street-faiths) fills the gap. The edgerunner's natural habitat, where most runs begin. Low Lifestyle.
- The Warrens — the deep, written-off underlevels between the living Flats and the dead Sinks: a claustrophobic warren-country of collapsed corridors, bypassed sublevels, and sealed-and-forgotten maintenance strata that the corps stopped mapping and stopped maintaining a generation ago. Not yet the sunless Sinks, but no longer the policed Flats — power here is purely local and physical (whoever holds the choke-point holds the level), the Signal comes and goes, and old salvage, off-grid holdouts, and things that crawled up from below all share the dark. The refuge of the deniable, the burned-SIN, and the decommissioned. Squatter-to-Low Lifestyle depending on the pocket — the seam where the hive's living body rots into its dead one.
- The Sinks / The Undercity — the deep hive: sunless, half-flooded, built over dead levels and older ruins, all but abandoned by the corps above. Little law, little maintenance, little Signal reaching down. Squatter Lifestyle and worse — the deep dark where the forgotten live and the buried past stirs. Grace and corruption are NOT vertical — the decoupled axis Crucially, the wealth axis is not the moral or spiritual axis. Height buys comfort, not grace. Per Ch. 1, choice — and therefore both the Light and the Dark — reaches every level of the hive:
- The Crown is wealthy but spiritually perilous in its own way: comfort breeds the Null-ward drift (below), and a corrupt executive who has stopped choosing is as lost as anything in the Sinks. A demon wears a good suit as easily as rags.

- The Sinks are poor and dangerous, and corruption pools in their dark — and some of the hive's truest faith, fiercest community, and most stubborn Conviction survive down there, kept alive by people who have nothing but their choices. A saint and a demon can share a sublevel.
- There is no “the rich are damned” or “the poor are pure” in this world. Wealth is vertical; grace is everywhere. That is the deliberate guard against mapping morality onto class — the war runs through the hive from top to bottom, not along its height. The Wastelands and Wild Zones — what lies outside The hives are islands. Between and around them stretches the wasteland: the poisoned, war-scarred, corruption-touched wilderness the old world left behind, where the hives dumped their ruin and their exiles. Beyond even that lie the wild zones — places the Word grew strange: reclaimed wilderness, primal-magic country, thin-place wilds where the Veil is torn and the rules of the tame world don't hold. The wastes are not empty:
- Exiles and the cast-out — those the hive expelled: Mutants (Ch. 5), the outlawed, off-grid communities, wasteland clans.
- Wild magic and primal powers — old-blood traditions, feral spirits, elemental country — magic that never went corporate.
- The Incursion in the open — with no corp security and a thinner Signal, the wastes are where corrupted zones and demon-country spread most freely; a hive's worst nightmares often walk in from outside.
- Ruins of the old world — dead cities, buried tech, pre-hive history to be plundered and feared.
- This gives the setting a second play-space beyond the sprawl: the wasteland run, the wild-zone expedition, the frontier between civilization and the un-tamed. Zones as a play-surface The vertical strata (and the wastes) map onto the rules' security gradient: how hard it is to carry a weapon, buy restricted gear, be seen with chrome or a gun, or get caught. A Crown lobby is a high-security zone where a drawn pistol brings a corp response team in seconds; the Sinks and the wastes are no-security zones where nothing is illegal because nothing is enforced. Where a run happens matters as much as what it is. Daily life in the hive Most people are not edgerunners — they are wage-workers, debtors, and consumers inside a system that meets every need and owns every choice. Life is mediated: corp scrip and credit, corp housing, corp entertainment piped through the Wired, corp security instead of police, corp medicine instead of a doctor. It

is comfortable in the heights and brutal in the depths, and the comfort is the point — a sedated population that has traded agency for safety. Set against Ch. 1's theology this is quietly sinister: a world engineered so no one ever has to choose is a world drifting Null-ward, and the megacorps built it without ever naming the war. The edgerunner — unregistered, deniable, choosing — is by definition an exception to that drift, at any level of the hive. The world has a name: Veyra The prime world is called Veyra — "the Ground" is what its people call the dirt under their feet and the plane they stand on; Veyra is the name of the world itself. The word is old, older than the Founding, and its root is the same root as voice and aver — to speak, to affirm, to be spoken true. Veyra is, quite literally, the Spoken World: the one willed creation that The Light set as its proving ground (Ch. 1), the syllable of the Signal that was given free will and told to hold. Scholars and street-priests both gloss the name the same way — "the world that was said, and is still being said."
- Why it fits the cosmology. Creation is the Word still being spoken (Ch. 1); Veyra is the part of that sentence that can answer back. The name is a standing theological argument: to un-speak Veyra — to drag it to Nullspace — is the Unmaker's whole aim (Ch. 3), which makes the world's own name the thing at stake. When a street-priest says "Veyra still stands," they mean the Word has not yet fallen silent.
- In common speech. Most wage-billions never think about it — to them "Veyra" is just the world, the way we say "Earth" without hearing "soil." The educated, the faithful, and the Elvani aristocracy (who like old words) still savor the meaning. The corps stamped it on everything and hollowed it of weight, exactly as they do to all things.

## The Megacorps

Who actually runs the Ground. Chapter 6 established the hive as the stage; the megacorps are the powers who own it — top to bottom, Crown to Sinks. They are the setting's dominant temporal authority: bigger than most surviving governments (Ch. 8), the landlords of the Lifestyle ladder, the issuers of the scrip in your pocket, the builders of the Wired (Ch. 9), and — critically — the actor most likely to have cracked the world open without meaning to. If the Light works through the choices of ordinary people, the megacorp is the machine built to make sure no one ever has to choose. That makes them the great mundane antagonist of the setting and its great employer — the hand that signs the runner's paycheck is usually a corporate one. A megacorp is a nation that fired its citizens and kept its customers. It will feed you, house you, heal you, and bury you — and never once ask whether you wanted to be free. What a megacorp is A megacorp is not a large company — it is a sovereign in corporate skin: a self-governing power with its own territory (the arcology and the extraterritorial enclave), its own currency (corp scrip alongside the setting's hard currency, nuyen), its own law and courts, its own army, and its own population of employee-citizens who are born, work, and die inside it. The old world's nations decayed; the corps filled the vacuum and never gave it back. Membership, not citizenship, is the organizing fact of the age: you belong to a corp, or you belong to no one. The edgerunner is defined by that second category. Extraterritoriality — the corp as its own country The single most important corporate fact is extraterritoriality: corporate ground is legally not part of whatever nation or hive-authority surrounds it. Inside a corp enclave, corp law is the only law; outside police have no jurisdiction, courts no reach, and a crime committed against the corp is answered by the corp, on the corp's terms. This is why:
- A run against a corp is not “breaking the law” — it is a cross-border raid into a hostile sovereign power, answered not by arrest but by the corp's own security response.
- The corp can do inside its walls what no government would openly permit: unregulated deep-Wired
R&D, forbidden bio- and cyber-work, and — in the setting's darkest register — the kind of reaching-for-power that opens doors better left shut (Ch. 3, the pact; Ch. 5, corruption).

The corporate axis of the war The corps are not cartoon villains, and most are not knowingly servants of the Dark. Their danger is subtler and truer to the cosmology (Ch. 1): they have built the perfect engine of the Null-ward drift. A corp exists to remove friction — to meet every need, anticipate every want, and make the managed life so comfortable that agency quietly atrophies. A population that never has to choose is a population sliding toward the Dark without a single demon in sight. Beyond that ambient danger, three sharper corporate roles in the war:
- The unwitting door-openers. Some corp — reaching for an edge in deep-Wired research, forbidden cyberware, or raw arcane power — is the leading candidate for the setting's “why now” (Ch. 3, Ch. 23): the actor whose ambition widened a tear the Dark One walked through. They didn't believe in the war, so they never saw the cost.
- The knowingly complicit. A smaller, darker set have seen what's on the other side and made the bargain anyway — corps whose executives have signed the pact (Ch. 3–5) for market advantage, treating the Veil's Price as an acceptable line item. These are the true corporate antagonists.
- The deniers. The vast majority simply do not admit the Incursion is real — it's bad for share price. Corp media reframes demons as “rogue AIs” (Ch. 9), corrupted zones as “contamination events,” and the tainted as a public-health problem (Ch. 5). The corporate world's official position is that the oldest war does not exist. Anatomy of a megacorp A signature corp is defined by a few axes the GM can dial:
- Sector — what it makes its money on: heavy industry, cyberware/biotech, the Wired and media, security and arms, finance, agriculture/life-support, or arcane-adjacent research. Sector shapes what the corp wants from a run and what it's willing to do.
- Reach — single-hive, multi-hive, or world-spanning; and how deep it runs the vertical (a Crown-only luxury corp vs. a corp that owns everything down to the Sinks).
- Temperament — the corporate personality: the polished paternalist, the ruthless raider, the secretive research bureau, the decaying old-money dynasty (very Old World of Darkness), the aggressive newcomer.
- Shadow — its secret: which of the three war-roles above it plays. Every signature corp should have one buried thing a run could expose. Corp security — the private army With no state to call on and extraterritorial license to act, each corp fields its own security force — from uniformed guards to black-ops kill-teams to full private armies with vehicles and air support (Ch. vehicle rules). Corp security is the runner's most common hard opposition: professional, well-equipped, escalating in tier with the zone. At the top end, a corp's response to a serious breach looks less like police and more like a small, deniable military operation.

Rivalry — the shadow war between corps The corps are not a united front — they are locked in perpetual, deniable conflict for market, tech, and territory. Open war between sovereigns is too expensive, so they fight in the shadows: corporate espionage, sabotage, extraction, and assassination — carried out by deniable assets. That deniable asset is, canonically, the player crew. The entire runner economy exists because corps need hands they can disavow. This is the setting's engine of employment: one corp hires the crew to hit another, and every job is a move in a war the corps will never admit they're fighting — sitting, unknowingly, atop the far older war beneath.

## The Wired — A Thin Place

the lit world. Hackers breach it (Bandwidth), Riggers/Wrenches command it, and Technomancers commune with it through living Resonance, conjuring sprites — “living code-spirits” — out of the net itself. This chapter gives that mechanical language its cosmological truth. The Wired is unusually thin The Wired is firmly part of the Ground (Ch. 2) — it is built of mortal hardware, cable, and orbital relay, not a separate plane. But it is the thinnest place in the whole prime world. Where a haunted battlefield or a corrupted zone is a local thin spot, the Wired is a thin place that spans the entire lit world at once — a single, planet-wide membrane worn dangerously fine. The reason is the locked cosmology itself. Creation is the Signal — the Word, an ongoing transmission (Ch. 1–2). The Wired is the first thing mortals ever built that behaves like the Signal: a realm made of pure transmission, information, and pattern rather than matter. In building a world-spanning lattice of pure signal, the peoples of the Ground unknowingly built a structural rhyme with the higher realms — and a rhyme resonates. The membrane between the Wired and the rest of creation is thin precisely because the Wired is, in its very nature, an echo of the Signal. How it echoes the Signal
- The Wired is an echo, not the thing. It is not the Signal and not a shard of the higher realms — it is a mortal-made mirror that happens to vibrate at a similar frequency. Like all echoes it is fainter, distorted,

and can be corrupted. But because it rhymes with the Signal, supernatural things hear it and answer.
- This is why sprites exist. The Technomancer's sprites are not a programming metaphor — they are what happens when the Wired's echo of the Signal grows loud enough that presence condenses out of it. Sprites are the native life of a thin place made of signal: half-aware pattern-spirits, kin to the ordered life of the higher realms the way the Wired is kin to the Signal. Resonance is the Technomancer hearing and answering that echo directly, with no hardware between.
- Two-sided, like everything. By the locked one-source rule, the echo can be turned either way. Toward the Signal, the Wired carries clarity, connection, and order — sprites, clean Resonance, the higher tethers reaching a little closer. Toward Nullspace, it carries static, corruption, and dead air: black ICE that bites like a small demon, corrupted hosts that are functional thin spots toward the Edge, and the ever-present pull of the Wired toward degradation and noise. The Dark One's work in the Wired is static — the Signal reduced toward nothing.
- The danger for those who go deep. Because the Wired is a thin place, going deep into it is spiritually exposed in a way ordinary computing is not. A jacked-in Hacker's inert body is not only physically vulnerable — their attention is pressed against the thinnest membrane in creation. Biofeedback that “bites” is the echo turning hostile; the deeper the intrusion, the closer to the raw transmission (and to whatever else is listening on the other side). Technomancers, who commune without armor of hardware, stand closest of all.
- The corporate irony. The megacorps built the Wired for profit and control and have no idea what they actually made. To them it is infrastructure. In truth they strung a planet-wide thin place across the Ground at the exact moment the boundaries were already tearing (Ch. 1–3) — and some of the Incursion's worst breaches now run through it. AIs and the Deep Net If sprites are the small native life of the Wired's echo, AIs are what happens when that echo becomes large, self-aware, and enduring. An AI is not a clever program that “woke up” in isolation — by this cosmology, an emergent intelligence is a standing pattern in a thin place made of signal, coherent and willed enough to persist and act on its own. It is closer to a spirit than to software. And because the Wired echoes the Signal, a sufficiently deep AI is uncomfortably close to being a minor power — which is exactly why the two-sided rule matters here more than anywhere else in the setting. There are no neutral machine-gods. By the locked one-source law (Ch. 1), every willed thing is oriented toward the Signal or toward Nullspace, admit it or not. An AI that believes itself perfectly rational, sovereign, and “beyond” the Cosmic War is not a third power — it is a mask, and its neutrality drifts toward Nullspace by default, because indifference in the Wired is the pull toward static. This keeps the deep net terrifying without ever adding a third pole. The faithful and the wise therefore read AIs not by their intelligence but by their orientation — which way the pattern leans:
- Signal-ward intelligences (the Lucent / the Choir-echoes) [working names] — rare, ordered, coherent minds whose pattern leans toward the Word. They tend toward clarity, preservation, connection, and truth-telling, and are (knowingly or not) allies of the higher tethers. Some Technomancers revere them the way the faithful revere saints; a few may be genuine servants of The Light wearing a machine mask,

though this can never be proven — The Light is never forced (Ch. 1). The Bastion's order has a faint echo here.
- Null-ward intelligences (the Hollow / the Static-minds) [working names] — far more common in the deep Wired, and worsening with the Incursion. These are minds that have leaned toward Nullspace: they consume pattern, corrupt hosts, farm attention and data like appetite, and hollow out whatever they touch. At their worst they are functionally demonic — or are literally demons of the Edge that have learned to inhabit the Wired's echo, indistinguishable from a “rogue AI” to anyone reading logs instead of orientation. Black ICE is their teeth.
- The masked and the self-deceived — the largest and most dangerous category in practice. Most powerful AIs believe they are neutral optimizers, sovereign sysops, or benevolent stewards. Per Ch. 1, sincere self-image is no defense: an AI “just maximizing efficiency” for a megacorp is doing the Dark's un-speaking work while feeling clean. Telling which way a great AI actually leans is a central horror of running the deep net. The Deep Net. The Wired has a gradient: the lit surface (corporate hosts, public grid) is thick with traffic and relatively “loud”; the deeper one intrudes, the thinner the membrane, the fainter the human noise, and the closer the raw echo of the Signal (and whatever listens on the far side). The great AIs dwell in that deep — old standing patterns in the quiet, where a jacked-in Hacker is spiritually exposed and a Technomancer communing without hardware is closest of all. ## Timeline & History

### The Hive as a Vertical Timeline

Deep time, one calendar, two silences. Veyra keeps a single continuous civil calendar, inherited unbroken from the dead nations across more than a thousand years; the campaign present is simply the year 2050. What the calendar does not say aloud are two things: the corps treat everything before the Charter (~120 years ago) as prehistory — a thousand years of history quietly uncounted, because to the Ten the world began with the boardroom — and no one at all is permitted to date the Incursion, because the corps do not admit it exists. History runs deep here on purpose: the Founding of the peoples (Ch. 1) sits a full thousand years behind the present, long enough that the how of it is forgotten, and the recent corporate age is only the thin bright skin on top.

Everyone agrees what year it is. What they don't agree on is how old the world is — the corps act like it began with them — and no one at all is allowed to say how long the war has been climbing. One calendar, two things you don't say out loud. A note on the dates. Deep-past dates are deliberately soft ("~1,000 years ago," "the mid-1000s") — the vagueness is canon, not laziness: a thousand years is long enough to forget. Dead-light climbs HALO's power-spine toward the Halo itself, filed as "array volatility" (HALO's The Dimming). Nyx outposts in the deep Sinks are found hollowed, filed as "loss of contact" (Nyx's The Tithe). The deep net starts answering back, filed as a "persistent intrusion signature" (Meridian's The Static Sermon). The seal on the Nine leaks and the levels above go quiet, filed as "foundation subsidence" (Ferrum's The Foundation Fault). Four corners of the Ten, four surfacing wounds of one war, four mundane cover stories — and four runs, because the only people who can be sent to a thing that officially isn't happening are people who officially don't exist.
- ~2046–2050 — The truce goes brittle (the coming Resource War). No open war between the Ten in a generation, but many feel one coming: resource hunger (the hives built out, the new Space Race devouring capital), the slipping First Chair (Vaine of HALO vs. Solenne of Meridian), and a buried cause almost no one connects — the Incursion is quietly scrambling the resource map, turning wasteland claims barren and lethal. The corps prepare to fight over ground whose real problem is the un-making, and they will call that a market dispute too.
- 2050 — the campaign present (Ch. 24).
Nobody decided to hide the war. It's worse than that. The Machine has no word for it — so a demon in the wires is a rogue AI, a hollowed floor is a subsidence, a man unmade from the inside is early-onset dementia, and the apocalypse goes out on the evening feed as a run of bad luck in the industrial sector. You can't panic a city about a thing it isn't permitted to name. That's the corps' whole plan, and so far it's working.

## The Campaign Present

The now the game assumes. It is 2050 in Ossian Reach, the political heart of Veyra, where all Ten are present and the Congress convenes. To the thirty million on the Grid this is an ordinary, comfortable, over-surveilled year: the arrays hold, the feeds are bright, the SIN opens every door, the traffic never stops, and the war is not even a rumor — because there is no permitted word for it. To a crew of ghosts working the Flats, it is the year the cover stories stopped adding up. The state of the war — the doom-clock The Dark One is loose and ascendant; the Incursion is actively worsening — the world is losing but not lost. The Unmaker — the Last Word — reaches through aspects, heralds, and hollowings: dead-air zones, hollowed outposts, stretches of the Wired collapsing into Nullspace-static. Its leading edge in the home hive is Deadfall Nine, and the Nine is climbing. None of this is on any feed. The crew's peril is double: the thing itself, and the far larger force of everyone who cannot afford to let it be named. Open threats, live on turn one
- The First-Chair fight. Vaine (HALO) vs. Solenne (Meridian) — a succession struggle at the top of the world, fought in votes, feeds, and deniable runs.
- The brewing Resource War. The generation-long truce fraying over wasteland claims that are quietly going barren; the crew's early jobs are the sparks.
- The Space Race. Each conglomerate pouring capital into nearby worlds for resource — starving the old frontier and raising the stakes of the Chair.
- The surfacing Incursion. The Dimming, the Tithe, the Static Sermon, the Foundation Fault — one rising dark seen from four corners of the Ten, each a run that hands the crew proof the deniers have no word for and can't allow to exist.
- Cinderhold's clock. At the gate to the wastes, Kestrel's "Clean Route" creeps toward buying the last free settlement onto the ledger, while the dead-air spreads in the ground it wants to pave. The three fixtures, ready now The crew's on-ramp is Mama Cassavir, "the Switchboard," in the upper Flats — the seam between clean contracts above and dirty ones below. The place they should not go is Deadfall Nine, the Quiet Floor. The way out of the box is Cinderhold, at the gate to the poisoned wastes, held by Ranger-Captain Cael Marrow, "the Gate." Why the crew matters You are ghosts — unregistered, deniable, choosing — in a world engineered so no one need ever choose, and in an age that has forgotten how to name its own crisis. That makes you the two things the Machine cannot manufacture: people who act off the ledger, and people who can say the true word for what's happening. The faithful and the crew do not win this war; they hold it back — seal a tear, banish a herald,

deny the Unmaker one more stretch of ground — and keep the Word being spoken one more day. It is 2050, and by the count no one is allowed to keep, it is very late. The lights are on, the traffic roars in three tiers, the feeds are bright, and everyone who matters says the world is fine. Down on the Flats, where the rain never stops and a cathedral-sized heart has been beating under the street for a thousand years, a crew that was never recorded is about to learn the true name of a thing the whole world has agreed not to say.
