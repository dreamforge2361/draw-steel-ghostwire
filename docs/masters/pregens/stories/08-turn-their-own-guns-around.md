# Turn Their Own Guns Around

*Kessic Draye — Hacker (Disruptor / offensive decker) · Mutant Human (Aberrant strain)*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · a corp sub-annex on the Grid, the crew stacked at a door they are not supposed to reach*

The others had a plan for the door. Kessic Draye had a plan for the building.

He sat with his back against the corridor wall, three feet from the breach point, and to anyone watching he looked like a corpse propped there to dry — thin, grey-skinned, still, a hood pulled low over a face that even friends found hard to hold a gaze on. A datajack cable ran from the port behind his ear to a scuffed deck in his lap, and his eyes were open and seeing nothing in this room at all. His body was here. Kessic was somewhere else.

He was inside.

"Talk to me, Null," Krow said over the crew-net — the big Cyborg planted in front of Kessic's slumped body like a wall, because that was the arrangement, that was always the arrangement: while the ghost is in the machine, someone guards the meat. A jacked-in decker was a bag of organs with the lights off. Everyone in the crew knew it. Everyone in the crew stood over him for it.

"Give me a second," Kessic said, and his voice came out flat and far away, the voice of a man reading a room he wasn't standing in. "I'm meeting the neighbors."

The sub-annex bloomed around him as a lattice of light — not walls and doors but nodes, every powered thing a glowing knot in the net: the maglocks, the camera runs, the two ceiling turrets folded up in their housings, the four smartguns the corp-sec team downstairs were carrying without knowing their guns had ears. That was the thing the wageslaves never understood about their own gear. Everything talks. Everything on the Grid was wired, and wired meant listening, and listening meant Kessic could lean close and whisper to it in a language it could not refuse to hear.

The outer ICE was cheap. He went through it clean — Intrusion, the break-in that was his daily bread, and it landed crisp on the first pass, no noise, the log staying quiet. He felt the little bank of throughput settle into him as he crossed the threshold, the deep-in-the-system feeling every decker chased: the further in you are, the more you can do. One node became a doorway to the next. He breached the camera run and it opened the door-bank, and the door-bank was slaved to the turret housings, and just like that Kessic was standing — in the only way that mattered — in the middle of everything they thought was theirs.

And nobody knew he was there. That was the whole art of it. Any thug could kick a server. Kessic could stand in the corp's own house with his hand on every light switch and scrub his own footprints as he went, so the Alert board upstairs stayed a calm and lying green. He was the man who owned the heat clock. The one thing the whole hive feared — the rising number, the trace, the moment the system notices — he held in his palm and turned down like a thermostat.

"Cameras are mine," he reported, flat and far. "Doors are mine. Alert's asleep and I'm keeping it that way. You want the room quiet or you want it loud?"

Krow's non-voice, dry as a fuse: "We want it over."

Downstairs, the corp-sec team came up the stair in a textbook stack, four shooters, smartlinked and confident, guns that put a targeting reticle in each man's eye. Confident men with smart guns. Kessic almost felt sorry for them. Almost.

He reached into their smartlinks all at once — Electronic Warfare, the disruptor's hammer, a wide wash of hostile static across every wired weapon in the stairwell — and their reticles died. Just like that. Four men who had trained their whole careers to shoot where the machine told them to, suddenly holding four expensive clubs, their aim gone to a bane, their comms shrieking. Then he did the thing he loved, the thing that was his, the offensive decker's signature cruelty: he did not just blind their guns.

He turned one around.

The lead man's smartgun was the nicest in the stack, and a nice gun is a talkative gun, and Kessic subverted it mid-raise — spoofed its owner-lock, flipped it hostile, and for one bright ugly second the weapon in the corp-sec leader's hands belonged to the grey man upstairs with the cable in his skull. It bucked once, on its own, into the shooter beside its owner. The stack came apart in screaming and confusion, and that was when the crew's own guns spoke, down the compromised hall, into an enemy that no longer trusted the hardware bolted to their own hands.

"Turrets," Krow said.

"Already ours." Kessic had Lockdown on the whole floor now — a five-count of pure control, doors sealing into walls the enemy couldn't open, the two ceiling turrets unfolding from their housings and swiveling to answer his commands, the elevators frozen, the exits that corp-sec wanted turned into a map that only opened for the crew. For as long as he held it, the building was not the corp's building. It was Kessic's. Everyone relying on a system he'd seized moved at a bane, in a house that had quietly, invisibly, changed sides.

He felt the host's ICE come awake then — real ICE, black and biting, a security decker somewhere on a higher floor finally noticing that the sub-annex had a ghost in it. It came for him the way it always came, a lance of hostile code reaching down the wire toward the soft jacked-in brain at the far end. This was the danger. This was the price of being in. If it caught him clean it would ride the cable back to a body slumped in a corridor and stop his heart in it.

But biting Kessic meant biting a man who was already wrong. The ICE hit the taint in him — the aberrant, off-grid strangeness the corps had made when they'd made him, the mutation that let him think in a machine-tongue no clean human brain could — and it hit Aberrant Resilience, the curse that also armored him, and it slid off harder than it should. He felt it as a cold jolt behind his eyes, a wound to nothing. He was still standing in their house.

So he bit back. The security decker was wired — of course he was, they always were — and a wired enemy was the one target Kessic could actually hurt with his own hand. He reached down the trace the man had opened and sent the biofeedback the other way, a feedback spike straight into an unguarded skull, and somewhere on a higher floor a man in a very expensive chair jerked and went slack and stopped noticing anything at all for a while.

"Clear up here," Kessic said. "They've lost the room. They just don't all know it yet."

It was over in ninety seconds, the way it was supposed to be when he did his job right — no alarm ever tripped, no reinforcement ever called, because the man who would have called them was a decker Kessic had put to sleep and a security system Kessic had owned. The crew walked out through doors that opened only for them and closed like they'd never been touched.

He came back to his body last, the way he always did — the pull up the cable, the sudden weight of a grey and aching frame, the corridor rushing back in around him. Krow was still standing over him, hadn't moved, a wall between the ghost and the world.

Kessic pulled the jack, coiled the cable, and got up without a word, because there was nothing to say that the log wouldn't have said better if he'd let it keep any record at all. He hadn't. That was the point.

Let them find nothing. Let them find no alarm, no trace, no wrongness — nothing but their own guns turned the wrong way and a green board that had lied to them all night. Let them stand in a room they thought was theirs and never understand it had belonged to someone else the whole time.

The best runs were the ones the target never knew had happened.

Kessic Draye was very, very good at making sure they never knew.
