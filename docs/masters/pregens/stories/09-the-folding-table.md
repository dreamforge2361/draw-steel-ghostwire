# The Folding Table

*Renn Solace-Ward — Medic (Street-Doc) · Pure Human*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · a service alley off Kell Row, the Flats, after the second shift*

The table was aluminium, it had one short leg, and Renn had shimmed it with a folded corp-sec citation for eleven months.

He set it up the same way every night: out of the service door, three paces into the alley, under the one sodium lamp the block committee had bribed the utility to keep lit. Lamp, table, stool, bag. Then the sheet — a real one, boiled, because the thing that separated a doctor from a man with scissors was whether the surface was clean. Then he sat down and waited for the Flats to bring him what it had.

It brought him a kid with a burst hand.

"Popper," the kid said, before Renn asked. "Cheap one. Went off in the grip."

"Sit." Renn snapped a light onto the rail and turned the hand over on the sheet. Two metacarpals gone to gravel, the web of the thumb opened to the bone, powder burn in a starburst across the palm. Nothing he hadn't closed a hundred times. "You're going to keep the hand."

"That a promise?"

"It's a diagnosis." He was already working. "A promise is what you get from people who want something."

There was a particular thing about being Pure Human in the Reach that nobody who wasn't ever quite understood, and it was not — whatever the enclave preachers said — about purity. Renn had no chrome because a doc with chrome was a doc with a lien. The moment you let a ripper put six hundred nuyen of Standard-grade hardware under your skin, you owed somebody, and the somebody always turned out to be the sort of somebody who eventually wanted a favour done quietly on an aluminium table at three in the morning. Renn had watched it happen to two men he'd trained under. Both of them had better hands than him now. Neither of them worked a block where the patients could pay in what they had.

So: no wire, no plate, no optics. Just twenty-nine years of stubbornness, a pair of hands he kept warm in his coat pockets between patients, and the flat animal refusal to stop that his grandmother had called *determination* and the enclave had called *staying power* and Renn had never called anything at all, because you don't name the thing you're standing on.

"You'll feel that," he said.

The kid didn't scream. That was the other thing about the Flats. Nobody screamed anymore; they went somewhere behind their eyes and came back when it was done. Renn talked the whole time anyway — about the weather, about the lamp, about the block committee — because a voice in the dark was a thing he could give away for free and he was not going to run out.

Forty minutes later the hand was closed, splinted, wrapped, and packed with a trauma patch he'd scavenged out of a crashed ambulance's kit three weeks before and had been saving for exactly this.

"What do I owe you," the kid said.

Renn looked at him for a second. Then he said, "Come back in six days and let me look at it. That's the bill."

"That's not a bill."

"It is if you don't come back."

The kid went. Two more came. A woman with a chem burn down the inside of her forearm, industrial, from a job she wouldn't name; Renn irrigated it and dressed it and gave her the antidote dose out of his own pocket, and she stood there afterward with her jaw set, furious in the way people get when they have been given something they can't repay. Then a Corran dock-hand with an abscess along the jaw that had been three days past the point where it should have been drained, and would have been four if Renn had charged him for the first one.

Around two, a man in a good coat came down the alley and stood at the edge of the lamp-light and did not sit.

"You're the one who works out here."

"I'm the one who works out here."

"There's a clinic on Ninth with a licence."

"There's a clinic on Ninth with a licence and a deposit," Renn said, "and a SIN-reader on the door, and a waiting list that runs to eleven days. I have a table."

The man in the good coat had an offer, of course. They always did. A bench in a back room under a corporate flag; a supply line that didn't run through wrecked ambulances; hardware in his hands — *good* hardware, surgical-grade, the kind that turned a competent doc into a great one — and in exchange a short list of nights each month when he simply would not be available to the block. Renn let him get all the way to the end of it. He had learned not to interrupt an offer, because the shape of what a man wants is easiest to see when he finishes describing it.

"No," Renn said.

"You understand what you're refusing."

"Better than you do." He wiped the sheet down and folded it and started packing the bag, because it was two and the second shift was done and nobody else was coming tonight. "I've got about eleven hundred people on this block. Three of them can afford Ninth. The rest of them have got a folding table and a guy who doesn't ask what the burn was from." He snapped the bag shut. "You're offering to take the table away and call it a promotion."

The man in the good coat looked at him a long moment, and left the way people leave when they intend to come back with something other than an offer.

Renn shut down the lamp. He took the citation out from under the short leg, unfolded it, read the fine on it one more time the way he did every night — eight hundred nuyen, *unlicensed provision of medical services*, issued fourteen months ago and never paid and never, at this point, collectable — and folded it up and put it back.

Then he carried the table inside, because it would be out again tomorrow, and there was a kid with a splinted hand who had been told to come back in six days, and Renn Solace-Ward was, whatever else the Reach thought of him, a man who was going to be there.
