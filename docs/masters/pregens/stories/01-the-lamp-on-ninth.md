# The Lamp on Ninth

*Vessa Corran-Dov — Street-Priest (Shepherd) · Corran*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · the Flats, first light*

The rain in the Flats never fell so much as leaked — a warm, chemical drizzle wrung out of the ceiling four levels up, where the Grid's runoff got tired of being someone else's problem and started being yours. Vessa Corran-Dov had stopped hearing it years ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. It ticked on the tin awning over her door, it beaded on the dead ad-glass across the alley, and it ran in a thin grey rope down the throat of the down-shaft at the end of the block, going somewhere no one on Ninth liked to think about.

She was already awake. She was always already awake.

Vessa was Corran — built low and wide and unbudgeable, the way the old corps had made her people a thousand years back to crawl the mine-throats and the crush-narrow service crawls where a taller body would have died folded in half. Homo sapiens fossor, the corp gene-registries still called her line, in the small print no one read. Squat, the Grid called her, when it bothered to call her anything. She had a stonemason's forearms and a face like a closed fist, and she wore the grey wool of no church anyone had licensed, because the only church that would have licensed her was the one she'd spent her life refusing.

She lit the lamp first. Always the lamp first.

It sat in the window of the storefront that used to sell filtration cartridges before the filtration company got bought, and got bought again, and finally stopped existing in the way corps stopped existing — not with a closure but with a silence. Now the storefront was a soup line four mornings a week and a place people came when they were out of every other place. The lamp was real fire. That was the whole point of it. Everything else on Ninth glowed — the overlay painted halos on the corp shrines and price-tags on the air and a soft, apologetic blue over the puddles so you wouldn't look too hard at what was in them — but the lamp was a wick and a cup of rendered fat, and it did not ask your SIN for permission to be warm.

She thumbed it alight and said the small words she always said, not loud. Let the ones who need it find the door.

Somewhere a long way above her, the Word had spoken the whole world into being and then, by every account she believed, gone quiet on purpose — because a thing you're made to love isn't love, and the Light would rather be doubted than obeyed. Vessa found that easy to believe. She'd been made to work. She knew exactly what it did to a thing, being made for a purpose. The miracle wasn't that the Light shouted. The miracle was that it didn't, and people chose it anyway, one cold soul at a time, on corners like this one.

Her Conviction was low this morning. She could feel it the way you feel a battery going — not empty, just thin, the quiet drip of faith that filled her back up overnight not quite keeping pace with the drawing-down. It had been a hard week. It was always a hard week. That was the arithmetic of the thing nobody told you when you took up the pact: the Light gives, and the Light asks, and the asking is the part that keeps you honest.

* * *

The first one through the door was Tomas.

He was eleven, maybe twelve, human, all elbows, with the specific grey pallor of a kid who ate when there was eating. He came in sideways out of the drizzle and stood dripping on her floor and would not look at her, which meant he wanted something and was ashamed of it, which meant it was about his mother.

"She won't wake up right," he said to the floor.

Vessa was already reaching for her coat. "Won't, or can't?"

"The card." He finally looked up, and his eyes were the worst thing she'd see all day, because they were hoping, and hope on a Flats kid's face was a wound. "The clinic reader wouldn't take her card. It said — it said unverified. She's got the cough, the wet one, and they wouldn't scan her past the door 'cause the card said unverified and I don't—"

"I know what unverified means." She did. It meant his mother's SIN — the number every citizen carried, the invisible key that opened the clinic door and the ration terminal and the transit gate and the whole warm lit world — had hiccupped, or lapsed, or been quietly flagged by some system three owners removed from anyone who could be asked why. In the Flats a SIN was a rumor about yourself that the Machine could revise at any time. Half of Ninth lived one glitch away from unverified, which was one glitch away from doesn't exist, which — if you were poor and sick and eleven years old on a Thursday — was one glitch away from dead on a clinic step in the warm rain.

"I don't have card money," Tomas said. "I don't have—"

"I didn't ask you for money." She got the door. "Walk fast. My legs are short."

* * *

She did not heal his mother. She wanted that understood, in her own head, always — she was not a machine you fed a prayer and got a cure. What she did was walk to the clinic on the Ninth-and-Fold corner, plant her unbudgeable Corran body in the doorway the reader wouldn't let the woman through, and not move.

"Ma'am." The clinic wasn't a corp clinic — it was a Caduceus sub-sub-franchise, a Grafthouse cast-off, one bored tech and a diagnostic arch and a policy engine that answered to a spreadsheet in a tower she'd never see. "The reader flagged her. I can't scan an unverified. It's not me, it's the—"

"System. I know." Vessa put a hand flat on the arch, gentle, the way you'd touch a frightened animal. She let a little of the drip go — not a spell you could see, nothing that would trip a sensor, just the warmth she carried banking up in her chest and going out, into the room, into the tech, into the tired air. Sense the Veil, the old ministries called it; she just called it paying attention. The tech was nineteen. The tech had been yelled at four times before breakfast. The tech was not the enemy. The Machine was very good at making the tired yell at the poor so the powerful never had to.

"Her name is Aline," Vessa said. "She's got two kids and a wet cough and she's standing in your rain because a number blinked. You didn't make the rule. I'm not asking you to break it." She reached into her coat and set a certified credstick on the counter — anonymous, cold, shadow-ledger money, the kind that didn't ask your SIN either. Payday from a job she didn't discuss. "I'm asking you to run her as a walk-in cash patient. No card. No scan. Your policy engine allows it. It just doesn't advertise it."

The tech looked at the stick. Looked at the small, immovable woman in unlicensed grey. Looked, for one second, at the boy.

"...Cash walk-in," he said, and turned the arch's reader off, and turned the diagnostic on. "Bring her through."

It cost Vessa forty percent of the run money and a favor she'd now owe the fixer who'd fronted the credstick. It bought a nebulizer course and a scrip and a woman who would wake up right tomorrow. The Light did not descend. Nobody's hands glowed. That was the shape of real faith on Ninth: it looked, from the outside, almost exactly like a stubborn person spending money she didn't have on a stranger, and refusing to move.

Which, Vessa thought, was probably the point of that too.

* * *

Mama Cassavir found her on the walk back, which meant Cassavir had wanted to be found.

You didn't reach the Switchboard; the Switchboard reached you, through a door you didn't know was a comm, in a voice that came up out of a dead ad-panel as you passed. "Preacher." The panel's broken glass flickered with the shape of a face and then thought better of it. "You spent my stick."

"I spent your stick." No use pretending. Cassavir knew where every favor on three levels slept at night. "A woman and a wet cough."

"Charity." The word came dry as dust. Cassavir ran the Flats the way a heart runs a body — everyone owed her, everyone needed her, and she kept the whole tangle of debt and rumor and grudge in a head that never wrote anything down. "You know what charity does to your Renown, Vessa? Nothing. Nobody's scared of the woman who gives it away. Nobody hires the soft one."

"Nobody trusts the hard one with their kids, either." Vessa kept walking; the panel's voice paced her, jumping panel to dead panel down the block. "You reached me for a reason. It wasn't to scold my accounting."

A pause. Even the rain seemed to hold it.

"There's a woman working the Fold-side blocks," Cassavir said, and something in the dryness had gone careful. "Calls herself the Choirmother. Feeds people. Real food, good food, more than you can afford, Preacher, a lot more than you can afford. Wards their kids. Prays over the sick and — here's the part — the sick get better. People are moving to her corners. Yours are getting thin."

Vessa stopped.

"You want me to tell you she's a fraud," Cassavir went on. "She's not. That's the trouble. I looked. Her faith reads true. She started like you — nothing, a lamp, a soup line. But somebody found her. Somebody with resources. There's a soft-voiced fella comes by from up the Spire, calls himself her mentor, and ever since he started coming 'round, her food got better and her sermons got — smoother. Comforting. The kind that doesn't ask anything of anybody." The panel's flicker leaned closer. "You know that smell, Preacher. The kind of church that gives you everything and asks you for nothing. You've spent your whole life not being that church."

Vessa knew the smell. It was the smell of the Cathedral of the Sun, four hundred levels up, where the Radiant Concord broadcast comfort to a billion souls and called it the Light — the loud, certain, purchased faith that was the exact photographic negative of everything the real Light did. The church that had a name for people like Vessa. The name was lapsed.

"She doesn't know," Vessa said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the pattern before, in smaller ways: a good soul, a real fire, and then a patient hand from above that fed the fire the wrong fuel one degree at a time, until one morning it was still burning and it was warming the wrong thing, and the woman holding the match would swear on her life it was the same flame. The Dark can't make a fire, her own pact-work had taught her. It can only lean over yours and change what it's for.

"No," Cassavir agreed. "She doesn't know. She thinks it's answered prayer. Might be the saddest thing on the level." The dry voice sharpened to its point, the reason the Switchboard had reached her at all. "Somebody's gonna have to walk onto the Fold-side and find out how far gone she is. Somebody who can tell a true fire from a counterfeit, up close, without getting warmed stupid by it. That somebody's got a lamp and short legs and forty percent of my credstick left to her name."

The rain ticked on the tin. Down the block, Vessa's own storefront glowed its one honest un-networked orange, small against the whole apologetic blue overlay of a world designed so no one had to look too hard at what was in the puddles.

Somewhere far below the down-shaft, in the dark the overlay didn't reach, something that had been a foundation once and was now only quiet listened to the rain come down.

"Tell the fixer I'm listening," Vessa said. "And tell her the charity stands. The woman and the cough. That's not for sale."

"Never thought it was," said Cassavir, and the dead panel went dead again, and Vessa Corran-Dov walked home to tend her lamp, low on Conviction, forty percent to her name, owing a favor she couldn't pay, built by strangers a thousand years ago to endure things no one was meant to endure —

— and choosing, one cold soul at a time, to do it anyway.
