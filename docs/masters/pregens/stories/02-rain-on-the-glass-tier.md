# Rain on the Glass Tier

*Kaïs Vahn-Estal — Elementalist (Stormcaller) · Elvani*

*A GHOSTWIRE story — the Grid, Ossian Reach*

There is a particular sound a corp gala makes at the end of the night, when the guests have gone and the caterers are stacking the good glass: a high, exhausted ringing, crystal touching crystal, the last note of money at rest. Kaïs Vahn-Estal had spent nineteen years learning to stand inside that sound and smile. He could still hear it, some nights, three levels down and a whole life away, over the rain hammering the polymer roof of a Grid tenement that had never once held a party.

He was, by design, beautiful. Not handsome — beautiful the way a blade is, the way a bill of sale is: on purpose, at cost, for someone. The corp that had commissioned his line called it Aesthetic-Class. The salons called it Highborn. The people in this stairwell, if they knew what he was, would call him a corp-doll and spit, and they would not be wrong. He had been a fixture in a patron-house in the Spires — a beautiful thing owned by a beautiful family, brought out to make deals warmer and rivals careless, and put back in his rooms when the lights came up. Ageless, they'd promised him. You will be lovely when everyone who bought you is dead.

He had believed, for a long time, that this was the best a thing like him could hope for.

The storm changed that. Not a metaphor — an actual storm, the kind that only happens up high where the hive's climate stacks fail: a pressure front rolling through the Spires' open galleries, and Kaïs standing at a window he was not supposed to touch, feeling the charge build in the air like a held breath. He had never been taught what he was, underneath the engineering. No one teaches the furniture that it can burn. But the charge answered him the way a dog answers a whistle it has been waiting its whole life to hear, and when the patron's daughter put her hand on the back of his neck the way she always did, the window behind him did not shatter inward.

It shattered out.

They did not call it magic, in the house. They called it a malfunction, and then a liability, and then they called the people who erase liabilities. Kaïs did not wait to be erased. He took the storm down the hive with him, one level at a time, learning on the way what the salons had spent centuries making sure his kind never learned: that the Veil is thin everywhere, not just in temples, and that a mind trained to read a room can be trained to read the charge in the air, the water in a pipe, the current in a wall. That beauty was not the only thing they had accidentally built into him.

Now he paid a fixer named Cassavir for the privilege of being no one.

The job was simple, which meant it was not. A mid-Grid data-broker had bought a piece of paydata he could not read and could not sell, and it was killing him — the seller had come back wanting it returned, and the seller had brought friends. Cassavir, who took clean contracts from above and dirty ones from below and connected the two for a fee, had offered Kaïs the retrieval because it needed someone who could walk into a corporate stratum and be assumed to belong there. You have the face for it, the old woman had said, not unkindly, over the hum of a dead switching-station wired back to life. Use the thing they built you for. Spite is a fuel like any other.

So Kaïs stood now in a Grid corridor that smelled of ozone and cheap noodles, wearing a coat that cost more than the corridor, watching four men take apart a data-broker's flat with the patience of people who have done this before and expect to be paid either way. He could have talked his way past them. That was the old tool, the built-in one, the one the house had sharpened. He was very good at it. He hated it.

He reached for the other thing instead.

It came the way it always came now — not as a bolt but as a gathering. He stood still and let himself feel the storm that a city always is: the current running in the walls, the water sitting in the sprinkler main above the drop-ceiling, the charge he could pull out of the very damp of the air. He held it the way he had once held a smile through a four-hour dinner, patiently, letting it build, letting it want, and the men did not notice him at all, because a thing like him is scenery until it is not.

The first he gave the water. The sprinkler main above the doorway did not so much break as decide to be somewhere else, all at once, and he went down under it choking and blind. The second and third he gave the storm proper — he opened his hand and the charge he'd been holding went to ground through the nearest thing that would carry it, which was the two men standing on a wet floor in conductive boots, and the corridor lit blue-white and the noodle-smell became a burning smell and they folded like the puppets the house had always said he was.

The fourth ran. He let him. He was not, whatever the house had made him, in the business of killing people who had stopped being a problem.

The data-broker was under his own overturned table, holding the piece of paydata like it might warm him. He crouched to his level — a courtesy, not a threat — and for a moment the old tool and the new one were the same thing: the face they built to make you trust it, over the storm they didn't know they'd built underneath.

"You're going to give that to me," he said, "and I'm going to give it back to the people it belongs to, and you're going to go somewhere the seller's friends can't count. That's the whole deal. There's no better one in this."

He gave it to him. They almost always did. It was the one part of the house's training he had not learned to hate, because down here he got to choose what he used it for.

Later, on the wet stairs between the Grid and the Flats, he stopped where the corp lighting gave out and the local stuff took over — the seam where up became down. Rain found its way even here, three levels below where rain should be, running down through the hive's wounded plumbing to fall on people who had never seen a sky. He held his hand out into it and let a small charge dance between his fingers, blue and quiet, just to know it was his.

Somewhere above him, in a Spires gallery with real windows and imported weather, a patron-house was still pretending it had merely lost a favored possession, and not that its favored possession had walked down into the body of the hive and learned to call the lightning. They would come looking eventually. Ageless cut both ways: he would still be here, still lovely, still furious, long after the daughter who'd put her hand on his neck was dust — and he intended to use every year of it.

Cassavir's voice crackled in his cheap earpiece, dry as ever. "You're on the news, Highborn. 'Freak power surge, Grid level forty-one.' Very tidy. You want the next one, or you want to go be beautiful and quiet for a while?"

Kaïs closed his hand. The charge went out. The rain kept coming down through the broken hive, the way it always had, the way it always would, indifferent to who owned the roof.

"The next one," he said.
