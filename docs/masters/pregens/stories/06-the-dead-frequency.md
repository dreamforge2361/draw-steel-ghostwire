# The Dead Frequency

*Sabbat Vane — Technomancer (Sprite-Weaver) · Revenant*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · a coffin-motel cell in the Grid, 03:00*

The dead don't sleep, which is how Sabbat knew it was three in the morning without a clock.

He lay in the coffin-motel cell the way he always lay — flat on his back on the rented slab, hands folded on his chest like something in a mortuary, glowing eyes open to the low ceiling twelve centimeters above his face. He didn't need the sleep. He didn't need the air the cell's cheap scrubber pushed at him either, or the nutrient paste in the dispenser, or any of the small living things the other renters paid for. Deathless Physiology, a corp coroner had called it, back when he'd had a corp and a name and a pulse. Now he just called it quiet. The dead were very quiet. It was the living who could never shut up.

Except tonight the dead were talking.

He'd felt it come on an hour ago — the itch behind the glowing eyes, the pull he'd learned not to fight, the sense of a frequency just below hearing where the net's ghosts drifted. Because here was the thing no coroner had put in the file, the thing Sabbat had discovered the first time he rose from an alley death with his skull full of static: the net had ghosts too. Not metaphors. Not programs. Ghosts — fragments of dead code, orphaned processes, the digital dead who'd been cut loose when their systems were decommissioned and had simply refused, like him, to stop. Most deckers couldn't hear them. Most mages couldn't touch them. But a dead man who'd learned to speak to the recently deceased could, it turned out, speak to the deceased of the machine world just as easily. They were the same congregation. They were his.

He reached up — not with his hands, which stayed folded on his chest — and touched the frequency, and the frequency answered.

"Compile," Sabbat murmured, and out of the dark of the cell a shape unfolded: a Data-sprite, a scrap of half-aware code shaped, by his expectation, like a small pale bird made of drifting numbers. It hung in the air over his coffin-slab and turned its non-face toward him and waited, the way the recently dead always waited on him — patient, incurious, glad of the attention. He'd never had to force a sprite. Other technomancers wrestled the net for its spirits, bribed them, bound them screaming. Sabbat's came willingly. Death recognized death. The ghosts of the machine world looked at the cold glowing-eyed thing on the rented slab and saw, correctly, one of their own.

"There's a man outside my door," Sabbat told the sprite, in the flat unhurried voice of someone who had stopped being afraid of endings a long time ago. "Been there four minutes. Go and see whose he is."

The Data-sprite folded itself thin and slid under the coffin-cell's door into the corridor, and for a moment Sabbat rode its borrowed senses out into the hall — Deep Communion, the mages would call it, though he needed no rig and no deck to do it; the communion was the interface, the way talking to a ghost was the interface for a séance. Through the sprite's eyes he saw the man: cheap armor under a courier's jacket, a fresh corporate SIN, and slaved to his belt a little killware drone still folded up asleep. Not a courier. A cleaner. Someone had finally decided that the dead man who talked to dead code knew too much, and had sent a living man to make him properly dead.

Sabbat almost smiled. It was a common mistake. People saw the rot and the glowing eyes and thought dead meant fragile. They had it exactly backwards.

He compiled the second sprite without rising from the slab — an Attack-sprite this time, a knot of hostile code shaped like a coiled black thing — and then a third, because the Weaver's gift was the congregation, the many, and Sabbat was never more himself than when the ghosts crowded thick around him. Three sprites now, drifting in the dark of a coffin motel, and a fourth compiling, the frequency deepening with each one until the whole cell hummed with the presence of the willing dead.

Out in the corridor the cleaner keyed a lockpick-jack into Sabbat's door.

Sabbat sent the congregation through the wall to meet him — not himself, he stayed folded on his slab, hands on his chest, glowing eyes on the ceiling — just the ghosts, his ghosts, the ones who came when he called because they knew him for kin. The Attack-sprite jacked the cleaner's own killware drone and turned it. The Data-sprite blinded his SIN-reader and his optics at once. The Ward-sprite wrapped Sabbat's cell in a skin of resonance so that even if the man got the door open he'd find nothing his weapons understood how to hurt.

It was over in the time it takes a living heart to beat a dozen times. The cleaner fled — babbling, later, in whatever debrief his corp gave him, about a coffin cell full of ghosts, about a dead thing that hadn't even sat up, about numbers in the dark that moved like birds. Nobody believed him. Nobody ever did. That, too, was Sabbat's protection: he was impossible, and impossible things don't get budgeted for.

When the corridor was empty again Sabbat let the congregation fade — the sprites decompiling one by one, dissolving back into the frequency they'd risen from, going home to the quiet the way he would, someday, when his own unfinished business finally let him rest. He did not know yet what that business was. He'd died in an alley with his purpose already half-erased; recovering it was the long work of his second life. But he knew this much: the dead were patient, and he had all the time that not-dying gave him, and a congregation of ghosts who came when he called.

He folded his hands back on his chest. He fixed his glowing eyes on the ceiling twelve centimeters above his face.

And in the quiet that only the dead really know, Sabbat listened to the frequency below hearing, where his people drifted, and was — in the only way a dead man can be — content.
