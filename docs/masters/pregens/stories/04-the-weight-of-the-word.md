# The Weight of the Word

*Barak Voss-Hallor — Commander/Face (Street-Fixer) · Goliar*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · the Flats, a shuttered foundry-floor*

The kid across the table was doing the math, and Barak let him.

It was good math to let a man do. The kid — Sover, a mid-tier fixer's runner with a jawline that had never been broken and a jacket that cost more than the room they sat in — had come to the shuttered foundry to renegotiate. That was the word he'd used on the comm. Renegotiate. He'd come with two shooters and a smile and the confidence of a man who had read Barak's file and seen the word Goliar and, under it, the word grunt, and had decided the second word was the one that counted.

He was doing the math now because Barak had come out of the dark of the foundry floor and stood up to his full height, and the math had changed.

Nine feet of engineered muscle was the first line of the equation. That was just breeding — the corps had grown Barak in a tank on the Kwan-Yang heavy line to lift what forklifts couldn't and break what charges wouldn't, and he'd have been a wall of a man on flesh alone. But the corps hadn't stopped at flesh. They never did, with the muscle. They'd laced his bones with reinforced weave and plated his torso in dermal armor and hung a hydraulic ram where his right forearm used to end, because a security Goliar that could also breach was worth two line items, and the accountants liked a two-for-one.

So the second line of the kid's math was the chrome. It caught the work-light in long dull grey planes across Barak's chest and shoulders, and it did not gleam prettily the way a Face's soft social suite gleamed. It looked like what it was: industrial demolition hardware, worn on the outside of a person, by choice.

That was the part the kid couldn't make balance. Barak watched him try. He's a Face, the file had said. A fixer. A talker. And every talker the kid had ever met kept the chrome hidden, or wore none at all, because everyone knew that visible metal cost you the room — it made people wary, it made them cold, it ate the warmth a smooth operator ran on. A fixer wrapped in demolition plate was a contradiction. It was a man who had thrown away the very thing his trade was built on.

Except.

The corp file had said he couldn't charm. Minus one Presence, the geneticists wrote, blunt in polite company — their clean way of saying the tank had made a weapon, not a diplomat. And the file, as the file always did, had gotten him exactly wrong. Because a big man who is smart — and Barak was smart — learns very early that the most disarming thing in the world is a large and obviously dangerous thing being gentle. His voice had come out of the vat warm. Warm and low and unhurried, a voice you wanted to lean toward, a voice that made a frightened man feel, against all the evidence of his eyes, that everything was going to be fine. Barak had learned to use the warmth the way another man used a smile — to open the door — and he had learned the other thing too, the thing that lived in the oldest part of the brain, below language, in the place that knows the difference between a large thing and a small one. He had learned that the warmth was only worth what the roar made it worth.

He had leaned into the thing he was. He had spent his own hard nuyen, after he walked off the corp leash, buying more of the chrome, not less. Because on him — nine feet, size Large, engineered to loom — the metal did not read as cold. It read as inevitable. Every plate said the same plain sentence the accountants had said about the crew that came to collect his salvage, once: this ends one way.

"You came to renegotiate," Barak said, and his voice was the warm one, the comforting one, the big-man-being-kind one, and it filled the foundry the way warm water fills a boot. He might have been welcoming the kid to a family dinner. "Sit. Say the new terms. Nobody here is in a hurry."

Sover glanced at his two shooters. It was a small glance, a check-in, the reflexive math of a man counting his own guns. Barak let him count. He kept the warmth on his face the whole time, patient and kind, a host waiting for a nervous guest to get comfortable — and that was somehow worse than a glare, because the kid could feel the size of the thing being kind to him.

Then the shooter on the left let his hand drift toward his coat.

Barak did not move fast. He simply stopped being warm.

"HAND." The single word came up out of him and hit the foundry like a dropped girder, the vocal modulator throwing it off the dead machines until it seemed to come from every direction at once, a wall of sound with the whole nine feet of him behind it, and the shooter's hand froze as though the air had gone solid around his wrist. For one full second the roar hung in the room and every man in it remembered, all at once and all the way down, exactly what they were standing next to. Then, into that ringing silence, Barak set his chromed right forearm — the ram, the breaching hardware, the thing built to open bank-vault doors — flat on the steel table, gently, and the table took his weight with a groan of protest, and did not break, and every man in the room understood in his spine that the not breaking was a choice Barak had made about the table and was, at this exact moment, also making about them.

"The terms," Barak said. And just like that the warmth was back, soft and unhurried, as if the roar had been someone else entirely — and the whiplash of it, the kindness returning to the same throat that had just shaken the room, was the most frightening thing the kid had felt all night.

And here was the trick the fixer's file would never capture, because it wasn't in any file — it was in him, the second half of the thing the corps threw away. Barak stayed in the warm voice now, and let it go warmer still, past reassurance, into something that sounded, impossibly, like care. Because the roar was the door, but he had learned — slow, the way he learned everything, and therefore permanently — that fear only opened the room. It did not close a deal. What closed a deal was the warm voice coming back afterward, the offer you made a frightened man to let him walk out whole, delivered in the same gentle rumble that had, thirty seconds ago, nearly stopped his heart. The thing about being the biggest, most obviously lethal object in any room was that you could afford to be generous in a way a smaller, more nervous operator never could — and your generosity landed all the harder for what it followed.

"You keep your original percentage," Barak said, to the frightened kid. "You keep your shooters. You keep your teeth. And you go back to whoever sent you and you tell them the foundry crew honors its contracts, and expects the same, and does not renegotiate — but does not hold a grudge, either, for a man doing his boss's errand." A pause, weighted. "That is a better deal than the one you walked in here to make. You just could not see it, over the chrome."

Sover looked at him for a long moment. Then — slowly, the way you move around a large animal you have decided is not, today, going to eat you — he nodded. And he took the deal, which was the old deal, which was the deal Barak had wanted all along.

They left. Nobody's teeth stayed on the foundry floor. That was, Barak reflected, standing alone again in the dark among the cold dead machines that had been built to do exactly what the corps had built him to do, the entire point.

An angry Goliar coming through the wall ended most negotiations early. Barak had learned to be the wall that didn't come through — the wall that could, and chose, visibly, expensively, in front of everyone, not to. That choice was worth more than any argument. He wore it in grey plate across his chest so no one ever had to wonder whether he had it. But the plate only made men wary. It was the voice that made them loyal — the warm rumble that welcomed them in, the roar that reminded them why they should not test it, and the warmth returning afterward, every time, like a promise kept. Men followed the plate out of fear. They followed the voice because some frightened animal part of them had decided, in the whiplash between the roar and the kindness, that this enormous grey thing had chosen to be gentle with them, and might not always.

The Corp had built him to break things and be pointed. He had kept the being-hard-to-argue-with. He had thrown away the being-pointed.

He ran his own board now. And when the crew sat down to a deal, Barak sat where everyone could see him, and spoke warmly, and welcomed them in — and let them all carry, somewhere behind the ear, the memory of what the warm voice sounded like when it stopped being warm.

It was, the others had learned, the gentlest voice in the Reach. Right up until it wasn't.
