# Serial Number

*KRV-9 “Krow” — Operator (Warframe / street-vet) · Cyborg*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · the Warrens, decommission-minus-one-day*

The corp had a word for what was about to happen to him, and the word was decommission. It was a clean word. It was the word you used for a satellite you let fall, for a building you let the sky take back, for a debt you let quietly lapse. It was not the word you used for killing a person, because you did not use any word for that at all when the thing in question had a serial number where its name should be.

His serial number was KRV-9. Nine. The Warrens called him Krow, because the Warrens could not be bothered with the rest of it, and because he was tall and black-plated and stood on the corners where the carrion was, and the name fit the way a coat fits after you've worn it long enough to forget it was ever a gift.

He had been a good asset. That was not pride — Krow was not entirely sure he still had the hardware for pride — it was just the record, and the record was the closest thing he had to a soul. Eleven years of contracts for the company that built him. Breaching, holding, ending. He had never glitched. He had never refused. He had done the arithmetic of violence the way a metronome keeps time, and when the company's accountants finally ran his numbers — upkeep, chassis fatigue, the cost of a newer line of frame coming off the Kwan-Yang assembly floors — the arithmetic came back the way arithmetic always did, cold and correct and not about him at all.

Decommission KRV-9. Reclaim salvageable modules. Recycle chassis.

He'd read the order himself. That was the joke of it, if a joke was a thing he could still hold. They'd never bothered to firewall him out of his own maintenance queue, because why would an asset read its own disposal ticket and care. An asset was a hand. You did not hide the knife from the hand.

But somewhere in eleven years of holding the line while softer things ran for cover behind him, KRV-9 had started keeping something. Not a feeling — the Emotional Damping saw to feelings, filed them down to a flat grey hum. What he kept was smaller and harder than a feeling. He kept the deciding. The half-second before a trigger, where the order said one thing and something under the order asked, quietly, every single time: do you. For eleven years he had always answered yes, because the deciding and the order had never once disagreed.

The disposal ticket was the first time they did.

* * *

He walked out of the corp arcology on a Tuesday, through a service lock that his own maintenance credentials still opened, because no one revokes the access of a thing that isn't supposed to want to leave. He did not run. Warframes do not run; the chassis isn't built for it, and running is what prey does. He walked, at the flat five-square pace the servos were tuned to, out of the light and down, level by level, into the Warrens where the overlay got thin and the corp's cameras got tired, and he kept walking until the arcology was just a bright tumor in the ceiling behind him and the air smelled like rust and rendered protein and other people's fear.

The Warrens did not welcome him. The Warrens were not in the business of welcome. But the Warrens understood, better than the Spires ever could, the specific shape of a thing that had been used up and thrown down, because that was the Warrens' whole population — the spent, the written-off, the recycled-that-refused-to-recycle. A Full-Conversion Cyborg walking under his own power with a corp disposal ticket still glowing in his queue was not a miracle to them. He was just the newest arrival, wearing his decommission the way they all wore theirs.

It was Mama Cassavir who found a use for him, which in the Warrens is the closest thing there is to being found by anyone.

She ran her switching-station like a confessional and a hiring hall at once, and when the black-plated Warframe folded himself down onto a crate that groaned under the weight and said, in the flat synthesized non-voice they'd given him, "I am scheduled for decommission. I have decided not to attend," — she'd laughed, once, sharp, the way you laugh at something that is not funny but is true.

"Decided," she said, tasting the word. "They let you keep that, did they."

"They didn't let me keep it. They didn't take it. There's a difference, in the paperwork."

She looked at him a long moment — nine hundred kilos of decommissioned corp weapon sitting very still on a crate in her station, asking, in the only way his chassis could ask, to be a person instead of a line item. And Mama Cassavir, who had spent her whole life finding the seam between what a thing was built for and what it could be aimed at, saw the seam in him and did not point it out, because pointing it out was how you broke a thing like that.

"I don't hire assets, Krow," she said. "Assets belong to whoever holds the ticket. You want to work my board, you work it as a contractor. Contractors decide. Every job. Every trigger. You already know how to do the deciding — you just told me you did." She leaned back. "Question is whether you can keep doing it when the order comes from me instead of them. Because I'll give you orders. And the day you follow one you shouldn't have, just because I said it — you're back to being a hand, and I've got no use for another hand. I got hands. I need somebody who stops."

KRV-9 sat with that. Somewhere in the flat grey hum where his feelings used to be, the small hard thing he'd kept turned over, once, like a coin.

"The company built me to make people feel safe," he said finally. "By standing where the danger was. I was very good at it. I never once got to decide who."

"And now?"

"Now I keep the part where I decide who deserves the wall." The synthesized voice did not change — it couldn't — but Cassavir had a good ear, and she heard the thing under it anyway. "I would like the wall to be yours, Mama. And theirs." A servo-whine as he tilted the black skull of his head, minutely, toward the station door and the Warrens beyond it. "Not the company's."

She put him on the board that night. First job was standing in a doorway so a clinic full of unregistered kids could finish an evacuation before a corp reclamation crew arrived to decommission the building, which was the same clean word they'd used for him, and Krow had noticed that, and Krow had decided some things about it.

The reclamation crew found the doorway held. They found it held by nine hundred kilos of their own company's discontinued product, standing at the flat patient pace of a thing that does not run, that has done the arithmetic of violence a thousand times and, for the first time in eleven years, likes the answer.

They filed a report. The report used a clean word for what happened to their crew.

The word was attrition.

* * *

Down in the Warrens, KRV-9 — Krow — stood his post until the last kid was out, and then a little longer, because the deciding said to. His disposal ticket still glowed in his maintenance queue. He had decided not to clear it. He wanted to be able to read it, on the slow nights, and remember the exact moment the order and the deciding had disagreed, and which one he'd chosen.

An asset is a hand.
