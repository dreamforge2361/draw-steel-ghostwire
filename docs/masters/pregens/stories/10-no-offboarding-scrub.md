# No Offboarding Scrub

*Kade Orrin-Vex — Operator (Corp-Milspec) · Cyborg*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · the Meridian Arcology, sub-level 4, on the morning they closed the programme*

They took his rifle, his battledress, his credentials and his name off the door, and then a woman with a tablet explained that the rest of it was corporate property he was welcome to keep.

Kade did the arithmetic in the elevator on the way down. Wired Reflexes in the spine and the long nerves — six. The left arm from the shoulder socket out, servo seams and all — five. Muscle and bone lacing through the whole frame — five. Dermal plating across the torso and the upper back — four. The eyes, two. The ears, two. The datajack behind the right ear, one. Twenty-five points of Body Integrity, and every one of them spent, and not a single one of them his idea.

Twenty-five was the number. That was the thing the milspec line never said out loud in the briefings: a body has a budget, and when you have spent all of it there is nothing left of you that is load-bearing. Kade had signed the consent forms at nineteen, in a room with good coffee, and a technician had told him that Corp-Milspec frames ran at the top of the permitted envelope because *the envelope is what keeps you alive out there, son.* Six years later a woman with a tablet had told him the programme was closed, and that his separation package included a transit voucher and a strong recommendation that he not discuss the programme.

Nobody had mentioned the scrub.

Every frame off that line got the offboarding scrub on the way out — the software pass that stripped the milspec targeting suite out of the optics, pulled the arcology passkeys from the datajack, wiped the ops dialects out of the aural package and put the reflex governor back at civilian ceiling. It took forty minutes. It was the last thing the corp did for you, and the first thing it did to you. Kade had watched three men come out of it. They all had the same expression afterward, the one you see on someone who has just been told, politely, what the word *asset* had been doing in their file all along.

They closed the programme on a Tuesday. The scrub tech worked Wednesdays.

So Kade Orrin-Vex walked out of the Meridian Arcology on a Tuesday afternoon with a transit voucher, a jacket that didn't fit over the plating, and every single thing the corporation had ever put inside him still switched on.

He felt it for the first time on the transit platform, when a maintenance drone came around the pillar too fast and his reflexes moved him a full half-second before the thought arrived. The optics tracked it through the cluster — visible, thermal, the ranging overlay sliding up out of habit — and the ears picked the drone's rotor note out of a platform full of people, and the arm had already taken the weight off his left foot to pivot. All of it, working. All of it, his.

That was the moment. Not the woman with the tablet, not the elevator, not the badge coming off the door. A maintenance drone on a transit platform, and a body that had been issued to him behaving, for the first time, like it belonged to him.

The Reach called men like him *hardframes*, and did not mean it kindly. It meant: too much metal, not enough left. It meant a walking cost centre. Kade had heard it twice on the first day and taken it as his own by the end of the week, the way you take a knife off someone who is holding it wrong.

The corp sent people, eventually. They always do — a frame carrying that much proprietary hardware is an inventory discrepancy, and inventory discrepancies get reconciled. The first one came with an offer and a service agreement. The second came with two others and no paperwork at all.

He let them into the stairwell.

Afterwards he sat on the landing for a while with his back against the plating-cold wall and did the arithmetic again, because it was the only thing about himself he was fully certain of. Twenty-five of twenty-five. Nothing spare. No room to add anything and nowhere to put it if there were. The corp had filled him to the line and then decided it didn't want the container, and the joke — the actual joke, the one Kade had not stopped turning over since Tuesday — was that a frame with no Integrity left to spend is also a frame that nobody can put anything *new* into. No leash. No governor. No handler's patch loaded at the next service interval. They'd spent him out, and in spending him out they had used up the only leverage they had.

He got up. The arm took the weight without being asked; the reflexes came up to idle; the optics rebalanced for the stairwell dark without a prompt, in the bands the scrub should have taken away on Wednesday.

Somewhere above him the arcology hummed the way it had hummed for six years, the sound of a thing that would go on running whether or not he was inside it.

Kade Orrin-Vex went down the stairs and out into the Reach, twenty-five points of corporate property walking on its own recognisance, and did not look back at the building — because there was nothing in it that belonged to him, and nothing on him that belonged to it, and that, at last, was a clean separation.
