# Nine Ways Out

*Vira Kellis-Nade — Wrench (Drone / Swarm-Master) · Changer, Wererat (Rat lineage)*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · a dead ventilation gallery under the Warrens, mid-run*

She had counted the exits before she counted the enemy. That was the first thing the tunnels taught you, and the tunnels had raised her: always know how you leave before you know why you came.

The job had come the way all her jobs came — through Cassavir's board. Vira had climbed up to the Grid for it, to the dead switching-station the old woman ran like a confessional and a hiring hall at once, all cold relays and a hundred silent lines wired back to a single humming heart. Mama Cassavir "the Switchboard" took clean contracts from above and dirty ones from below and connected the two for a fee, and she wrote nothing down; she just looked at Vira over the hum and said, Somebody's paying to have a thing that lives in your tunnels stop living there. You're the one who knows the tunnels, sweet. It was the same board Vessa's charity ran through, and Kaïs's spite, and the dead man Sabbat's ghosts, and Krow's stubborn refusal to be decommissioned — all of them routed through Cassavir's one dead station, none of them ever quite in the same room. Everyone owes her. She guards a crew's anonymity over any client. Vira had taken the stick, and gone back down where she belonged.

Vira Kellis-Nade lay flat in the crawlspace with her cheek against cold ferroconcrete, and the Warren-quiet hummed in her back teeth — the low, wet drone of a level that the corps had written off and the overlay had stopped painting years ago. Down here the AR went thin. Down here the cameras were tired. Down here she was home.

Getting into the gallery had been the easy part, and she'd done it the way her line always did — not through a door. Two levels up there was a service-duct grille too tight for any grown thing to pass, which was exactly why she'd chosen it. She'd crouched in front of it in the dark, breathed out, and let the change take her — a fast, fluid thing, no ritual to it, just a shrug of will and a maneuver's worth of time: her spine shortened, her clothes and her drone-case webbing folding away into fur and the drop of her own weight, until what pressed up to the grille was not a woman but a long grey rat with a tech's clever eyes. Beast Form. She'd oozed through a gap a fist couldn't fit, dropped four feet into the duct on the far side, and scurried — silent on the filth-slick metal, sure-footed where a human would have clanged, reading the black by smell and the low animal light of her own eyes. She'd come out into the dead gallery, found her crawlspace, and only then breathed back up into her own shape, the case reassembling around her as she rose. No door had opened. No sensor had blinked. The Warren had simply swallowed a rat and coughed up an operator.

Nine ways out. She had mapped them on the way in, the way her line always did — the grille she'd come through as a rat, the gnaw-hole she'd widened last month with a cutting torch, the down-shaft she could drop into and never touch a wall, six more. Nine. She never worked a room with fewer than three, and she was proudest of the nights she used none of them.

"Contact," Cassavir said in her ear, dry as a blown fuse. "Two shooters, coming your gallery. They know you're down here, sweet. They don't know where."

"They never do," Vira said, and opened the case.

It wasn't a weapon. People always expected a weapon — a rat-thing in a crawlspace, they pictured teeth. What Vira Kellis-Nade carried was a hardshell flight case the size of a footlocker, and when the lid came up it exhaled a soft blue glow and the smell of warm electronics, and the swarm woke up.

Four of them. Palm-sized quadrotors, matte and scuffed, each one a machine she'd built with her own hands from grey-market parts and salvage and spite. She didn't deploy them so much as let them go — a flick of will down the control rig seated behind her ear, and they lifted out of the case one after another, spun up, and fanned into the dark gallery like a breath held too long finally let out.

She felt the room bloom open in her head. Not her eyes — theirs. Four sensor-feeds stitched into one wide picture behind her forehead: the two shooters edging up the gallery in a covering stagger, the heat of them, the cheap corp-issue smartguns, the way the near one kept glancing at the ducts because someone had warned him about the ducts.

Good. Let him watch the ducts. The ducts were where she wasn't.

"Spread nine," she murmured, and the swarm obeyed as one thought — Wide Band, her rig fanning the whole fleet on a single command, the way her line had always moved: not one rat in the wall but rats in every wall at once, so that a hunter never knew which gap the bite came from.

The first shooter fired at a drone. Of course he did — it was the thing he could see. The little quadrotor took the hit, staggered in the air, Integrity gauge dropping in the corner of her mind — and Vira felt nothing, because that was the whole grace of it, wasn't it. The body they shot was never hers. She had a dozen bodies. She could build more. The thing they never understood about cornering a rat was that you had to corner all of her, and she was, at that moment, in five places, and none of them was the crawlspace with her actual soft and mortal spine.

"Focus," she said.

Every drone still flying turned on the near shooter at once — the swarm concentrates — and the gallery lit up with a stuttering crossfire of drone-guns from four angles no cover could answer, because cover assumes the shot comes from a direction, and Vira Kellis-Nade did not believe in a direction. He went down yelling into his own comm.

But she'd read the room a half-second wrong, and the Warren punished that. There'd been a third — not on any of her feeds, pressed into a maintenance alcove behind her crawlspace, and he came out of it fast and close with a stun-baton already swinging, close enough that no drone in the air could turn in time. Close enough to reach the soft mortal spine.

So she stopped being soft.

The change came as a free thing this time, the way it always did when the body knew it was about to be hurt — not a chosen maneuver but a reflex the danger itself triggered, faster than thought. She surged up out of the crawlspace already turning: shoulders broadening, hands going to claw, fur and muscle roping up her forearms, teeth crowding her jaw — Hybrid Form, upright and human-shaped and unmistakably not human, the combat shape her line wore when the walls stopped being enough. The baton came down where her skull had been. It found her half-turned instead, and here the rat's oldest gift answered: a hard twist away at the exact wrong-for-him instant, the blow skidding off a rolling shoulder that took half of what it should have — gutter reflexes, the survivor's flinch that never quite lets the hit land clean. She let the momentum carry her inside his reach, and a clawed hand closed on the front of his vest, and up close, in the dark, with her Feral Tell bared on purpose — the animal eyes, the wrong teeth, the low sound in her chest — she let him see what he'd cornered. He froze. People always froze. It bought the half-second she needed to put him down.

Then she breathed, and let the Hybrid ebb back into a woman's shape, because you didn't fight in the animal any longer than you had to. The drones were still up. The room was hers again.

The second one did the smart thing. The second one ran.

She let him. She watched him through her fleet's eyes all the way to the gallery mouth, tracked the exact door he chose, filed it — that's a tenth way out, note it — and did not fire, because a runner who lives to tell his fixer "she's got a swarm and she's in the walls, don't send me back down there" was worth more to Vira alive than dead. Reputation was a kind of tunnel too. It let you leave a room without ever being in it.

"Clear," she breathed, and called the swarm home. They settled back into the case one by one, warm and scuffed and hers, and she stripped a part off the drone that had taken the hit and clicked it into the one that hadn't — salvage, always salvage, the tunnels' oldest rule — before she closed the lid.

Then she lay in the dark a moment longer, cheek to the cold, and counted the exits one more time out of pure habit. Ten, now.

She took the gnaw-hole. She always took the gnaw-hole. It let out three levels down and half a district over, through a gap a corp-sec officer would swear no person could fit — and a person couldn't. So a foot from it she shrugged down into the rat again, one last easy maneuver, the case folding away into fur, and fit through it anyway on four quick feet, because that was the other thing the tunnels taught you, the thing under all the others:

They can build the box. You just have to be smaller than they think, in more places than they can watch, with one more way out than they've counted.

She was always smaller than they thought.

She always had one more.
