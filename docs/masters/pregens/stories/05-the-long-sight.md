# The Long Sight

*Wren Sable-Corvin — Scout (Hunter) · Changer, Wereraven (Raven lineage)*

*A GHOSTWIRE story · the roofline above the Grid, an hour before the meet*

From up here the Hive looked almost honest.

Wren lay flat on the cold ceramic lip of a Grid-tier maintenance spar, four hundred meters above the street she'd been paid to watch, and let the wind do what it wanted with her coat. She'd found the perch an hour ago the way she found all her perches — she'd gone up as a bird, circled once on the thermal that always rose off the coolant stacks at dusk, and picked the ledge with the cleanest sightline and the worst approach. The worst approach for anyone coming at her. The best approach was always straight down, and straight down was hers alone.

That was the thing the ground people never understood about a Wereraven. They thought the gift was the flying. It wasn't. Anyone with a rotor-drone and a spare afternoon could get height. The gift was the eye — the long, patient, raptor-tuned eye the change had left even in her human face, the eye that could read a man's shoulders from four hundred meters and know, before he knew it himself, that he was about to reach for a gun.

She was reading shoulders now.

The meet was set for a plaza three tiers down, a false-marble apron outside a corp-front noodle house that the fixer had sworn was neutral ground. Wren had stopped believing in neutral ground the first time she'd watched a neutral ground fill up with shooters from a hundred meters up while the marks below smiled and shook hands and had no idea. Neutral ground was a story the ground told itself. From the air there was no such thing — only the ground you could see all of, and the ground you couldn't.

She could see all of this one.

Her crew's client — a nervous Grid-tier data-broker with a briefcase and a bad haircut — was already in position at a café table, doing the small brave things frightened people do to look calm. Two tables over: a couple, too still, not talking, coffees untouched. Corp muscle, plainclothes. Wren had marked them the moment they sat. Marked — she felt the little internal click of it, the study-and-tag her whole trade turned on, the way her attention seized a target and did not let go. She knew the woman's lowest guard now the way she knew the wind. She could put a round through the gap in the man's cheap armor from here, in this wind, and she would not miss, because missing was for people who guessed, and Wren did not guess. Wren watched, and watching, done long enough and patiently enough, stops being a guess and becomes a fact.

But she wasn't here to shoot. She was here to see, and to tell her crew what she saw, half a second before it mattered, which in her experience was the whole of the difference between a job and a funeral.

She keyed her throat-mic. "Two watchers, plainclothes, the couple at your eight," she said, low. "Corp, not street — they've got the posture. They're not here for your client. They're here for the briefcase, which means they already know what's in it, which means your fixer talked." A pause while her long eye swept the plaza edge, the rooflines, the stairwell mouths — the reflex of a creature that has spent its life being small prey to bigger things and has decided, permanently, never to be surprised again. "Third one. Stairwell northeast, hasn't come out yet. He's the closer. When the couple stands, he moves. That's your tell. You've got about nine seconds after they stand."

"Copy," her crew lead breathed. "Can you take the closer?"

Wren considered the stairwell mouth, the angle, the wind, the drop.

"I can do better than take him," she said. "I can make sure nobody has to."

Because here was the second thing the ground people didn't understand, the thing even her own crew sometimes forgot: the Wereraven's real weapon wasn't the rifle laid cold and quiet along the ledge beside her. It was the warning. A shooter who knows he's seen is a shooter doing math instead of shooting. Wren had ended more fights by being visibly, undeniably watching — a single glint of a scope from an impossible height, a shadow with wings crossing the light at the wrong moment — than she had ever ended with the trigger. The corp closer in the stairwell was about to learn that the plaza had a ceiling, and that the ceiling had an eye, and that the eye had already found him.

She let the last of the dusk-light catch her scope, once, deliberately, angled down into the stairwell dark. A flat silver wink. I see you.

In the stairwell, the third man went very still.

The couple did not stand. The briefcase changed hands. Wren's nervous little client walked out of the plaza on his own two legs with his bad haircut and his data intact, and never once looked up, and never knew that the reason he was alive was lying flat on a cold ledge four hundred meters over his head, watching, the way she was always watching, the way the long sight had taught her she would always have to.

When it was done she didn't take the stairs. Scouts who take the stairs get met on the stairs.

She rolled off the ledge into the dark and the drop, and somewhere in the first half-second of falling the change took her — bones going hollow and light, the coat becoming wings, the long human sight folding down into the older, sharper thing it had grown out of — and Wren Sable-Corvin caught the coolant thermal on a black spread of feathers and climbed, unhurried, back up into the only tier of the Hive that was ever really hers.

The high one. The one you could see all of.
