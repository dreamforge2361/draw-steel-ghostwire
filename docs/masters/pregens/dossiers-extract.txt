                               Welcome to the Reach
   Come up out of the transit tube on the Flats and the first thing that hits you is the weight of the light. It
   doesn't come from a sky — there is no sky down here — it comes from everywhere at once: a hundred
   thousand signs stacked forty stories deep, noodle-stall holos and blessing-tier ads and simsense come-ons
   in six languages, all of it bleeding pink and cyan and sodium-gold into the standing rain. The rain never really
   stops on the Flats. It falls from the condensation of a million air-scrubbers on the levels above, warm and
   faintly chemical, and it catches the neon and drags it down the girders in long smeared ribbons so that the
   whole street looks like it's melting. Steam boils up from the grates. Somewhere below your feet a
   foundation-pump the size of a cathedral has been beating like a heart for a thousand years. Somewhere
   above you, past the Grid, past the Spires, past where anyone you know will ever stand, the Halo burns —
   HALO Ascendant's ring of orbital-fed daylight crowning the top of the hive like a second sun, so bright and so
   far that from down here it reads as a rumor of morning that will never actually arrive.

   This is Ossian Reach. The founders named it for what it was meant to be: a bone-tower that reaches, raised
   on the fossilized levels of everything it was built over, climbing from the drowned dark of the Sinks all the way
   up toward that engineered sun. To live in the Reach is to spend your whole life somewhere on that climb —
   and everyone, from the First Argent in her three-hundred-year-old vault to the SIN-less kid boosting drone
   parts two levels below you, knows exactly which rung they're standing on. Altitude is the class system here. It
   was designed that way before it was ever built in steel.

   Read the hive top to bottom and you read a life sentence. The Halo and the Spires are the sunlit apex — clean
   air, real light, executives who have never once seen the world they own, and who intend to keep it that way.
   The Grid is the vast wage-working body of the hive: patrolled corridors, licensed commerce, the salaryman's
   stable flat under artificial daylight, everyone one missed payment from falling. Then the corp light thins, and
   you come to the Flats — dim, dense, semi-lawless, where corporate authority gives out and gangs, fixers,
   level-bosses, and street-faiths fill the gap. This is the edgerunner's country, and it's where nearly every job
   starts. Below the Flats sprawl the Warrens, the written-off deep-underlevels the corps stopped mapping a
   generation ago, where power is purely physical — whoever holds the choke-point holds the level — and the
   Signal comes and goes. And beneath even that lie the Sinks: sunless, half-flooded, built over dead levels and
   older ruins, where Nyx's moving black market drifts through the dark and the buried past stirs. The way down
   is where the campaign's dread lives. The way out, through the outer gate to the poisoned wastes, is the only
   unowned ground left.

           The overlords, and the ones who walk
                           behind
   Nobody governs the Reach. Ten conglomerates own it, and a seat among them is the government. They call
   themselves the Congress; the street calls the whole apparatus the Machine. HALO Ascendant sells you the
   sun and the very air you breathe, rented back by the lumen. Ferrum Dynastic sells you the ground you stand
   on — it built the hive's bones and has known for generations what settles in the deep dark, and paid to keep it
   quiet. Caduceus rents you back your own flesh, dose by dose. Argent Exchange owns money, and therefore
   owns everyone in debt to it, which is nearly everyone — its Elvani credit-dynasties are patient in a way only
   three-century lifespans allow. Obsidian Holdings sells the perfect escape, forever, to anyone who can pay.
   And around them turn the rest — Meridian, Ironclad, Verdant, Sanctum, and the hungry, precarious Nyx Cartel,
   the legitimized underworld that runs the Sinks and the smuggling routes the other nine pretend they never
   use. Between them, the Ten own everything with a name: every citizen, every SIN, every soul on the ledger.

   And there is one power that is not a conglomerate but wears the shape of one. The Radiant Concord — the
   corporate church, the Cathedral of the Sun — broadcasts luminous comfort across the Wired to a billion
   frightened souls and sells them salvation by subscription. It is the most trusted brand in the hive. It is also the
   single most dangerous lie in the setting, because the loud, certain, purchased comfort it preaches is the exact
   photographic negative of everything real faith does — and its soft-spoken inquisitors, the Choir of the Silent
   Sun, exist to find the genuinely faithful and counsel them into silence. In the Reach, the gentlest hand has the
   coldest grip.

   Because here is the secret the Crown spends a fortune denying: there is a war on. Not the corporate cold war
   of hostile takeovers and deniable hits — an older one, breaking up through the floor. The world is a Word still
   being spoken, and something is trying to un-speak it, and it is climbing the foundations one dead level at a
   time. Most of the thirty million souls stacked in this tower will live and die calling that a rumor. The comfort is
   engineered precisely so they never have to feel it. A population that never has to choose is a population
   drifting quietly toward the dark — and the Machine built that world without ever once naming the war.

       The Wired — the overlay over everything
   Lay a second city over the first one, made of light and thinner than a breath, and you have the Wired. It is the
   overlay: the planet-spanning web the Machine runs on, and the thing every screen, every credit chip, every
   door-lock, every SIN, every rented lumen and metered dose and blessing-tier subscription is quietly wired
   into. Nothing in the Reach happens off it. The scrip in your pocket is a line in the Wired. The camera reading
   your face is a line in the Wired. The sermon pouring out of the Cathedral of the Sun and the turret tracking
   you across a corp lobby are the same web wearing two masks. To live in the hive is to be a node whether you
   consented or not — every citizen a lit point on a map, every soul on the ledger a record somebody up the
   tower can read, freeze, or erase. This is what it means that the Ten own everything with a name: the naming is
   the Wired, and the Wired is total.

   And here is the part the corps that built it still do not fully understand: the Wired is not just infrastructure. It
   is the thinnest place in the whole world — a membrane that echoes the Signal, the far-off transmission the
   whole cosmos is spoken from. The ancestor-corps raised the resonant chamber without ever grasping what
   it would resonate with. That is why a technomancer can reach into the deep-net with no hardware at all and
   have it answer back; why the dead net has ghosts in it. It is also why the deepest runs are the most
   dangerous work in the setting, because when you jack past the lit corporate surface into the deep layers, you
   are not just breaking into a database — you are pressing your bare mind against the thinnest wall in creation,
   and things live on the other side.

   The web is patrolled. Three kinds of mind move through it, and on a bad run you cannot always tell which one
   has you. First are the AIs the corps built on purpose — watchdogs, gatekeepers, and hunter-programs that
   guard the Machine's secrets, and the black ICE that answers a break-in by reaching back down the
   connection to stop your heart. Second are the sprites, the strange half-alive patterns a technomancer
   coaxes out of the echo — companions that can carry a message, chew through a lock, or stand between you
   and something worse, loyal in a way no program should be able to manage. And third are the ones nobody
   built and nobody wants to name: the minds that have gone wrong in the dark spots — presences that drift
   toward the silence, hollowing out the hosts they infest, biting with the same black ICE and leaving dead air
   where a living system used to be. The corps file them as rogue AIs and quarantine the sector. Some of them
   are not AIs at all. Some of them crossed in — the war (below) found the widest door in creation and walked
   through it — and you cannot tell a fallen program from a demon wearing the net as a body by anything it
   does, only by what it is, and only if you can read it at all. A friend on the wire and the thing that will unmake
   you can wear the exact same face. Learning to tell the difference — and knowing that sometimes you can't —
   is the whole art of running deep.

                           The peoples of the Ground
   The faces in the crowd are not all one kind of human, and none of them chose the shape they were born to. A
   thousand years ago the ancestor-corps engineered castes for purpose: the Elvani, slender and slow-aging
   and unnervingly graceful, built to be seen in the boardroom — a bespoke aristocracy the old wealth could
   commission. The Corran, compact and immensely strong, built to work the deep mines and the cramped
   crawls; the corps still read them as "just labor." The Goliar, towering and engineered for heavy industry and
   enforcement, still underestimated as "just grunts." Over ten centuries those castes bred true and became
   peoples — with guild-clans and salons and kinship-crews and long memory — and the mutual contempt bred
   true right along with them. Beside them stand the base-stock Humans the Word first seeded, and the made
   peoples, laid over a living person within a single lifetime: the Changers, whose metamorphic strains run
   wereraven and wererat and worse; the Mutants, in whom the Dark's corruption has settled into the blood and
   who carry a mark that will not simply wash away; the Revenants, the embodied dead who frighten the tidy
   Crown; and the Cyborgs, replaced so thoroughly by un-speaking metal that they are no longer, legally or
   biologically, called human at all. A person today is not a product. They are the descendant or the survivor of
   one — which is a very different, and much more human, thing.

   Power in the Reach comes through three doors, and all three draw on the same single source. The mage grips
   a strand of the stuff creation is made of and shapes it — fire, stone, storm, void — for a few violent seconds.
   The priest is lent a little of the Word through faith and sacrifice, and can have it withdrawn, which is exactly
   why the faithful can lose. The technomancer listens to what the machine echoes in the deep-net, where
   worse things wait in the dark spots. And then there are those who reach through no door at all — the ones
   who trade living flesh for chrome and firepower, narrowing the channel to power in exchange for the strength
   to walk through a firefight and out the other side. Three doors, one house. The mage shapes it. The priest is
   lent it. The technomancer listens to its echo. And the wired street-samurai spends their own soul's bandwidth
   for muscle and steel.

                         You are a ghost on the wire
   You are none of the thirty million. You are a ghost — a person the system never fully recorded, no SIN, no
   citizenship, no proof you were ever spoken into the world at all — and the wire is what you jack into, the
   chrome you run on, the deep-net you work. To be a ghost is to be unrecorded, undeniable, and free in the
   only way left in a world owned wall-to-wall. Your crew takes the work the corps can't be seen doing: the theft,
   the extraction, the sabotage, the ugly truth somebody up the tower needs buried or brought to light. You
   move through the sprawl like a rumor the Machine can't quite prove. The Ten deny there's a war eating the
   world from below, and most nights you'll take their money to help them keep that secret.

   Your crew might be a Corran Street-Priest working a Flats block with a lamp and real, costly faith; an Elvani
   Elementalist who calls the storm down through the rain; a decommissioned Cyborg Operator built as a
   corporate warframe and walking free; a Goliar Commander/Face who talks a room down before it ever comes
   to guns; a wereraven Scout reading the rooftops through a rifle scope; a Revenant Technomancer who
   summons the ghosts of the dead net from a coffin-motel cell; a wererat Wrench fighting through a self-built
   swarm of drones in the Warrens; a Mutant Hacker who seizes a corp's own turrets and turns them around.
   Whoever you are, you are the exception the sedated hive was built to prevent: someone unregistered,
   deniable — and choosing.

   This is the promise GHOSTWIRE makes you. You will not be a chosen one, and you will not be clean. You are
   the heroes and the anti-heroes of the Reach at once — the hands that steal for a corp on Monday and shatter
   it on Friday, that take the blood money and then spend it holding one dark level of the tower against the thing
   climbing up from below. You will make hard bargains at a real price. You will do a good thing for a bad reason
   and a bad thing for a good one, and the game will never once let you pretend you didn't choose. Because in a
   world engineered so no one ever has to choose, the ones who still do are the only thing standing between the
   Word and its unmaking.

   Welcome to the Reach.

      What this file is. This is GHOSTWIRE's collection of immersive fiction and pregenerated-character dossiers —
      short reads meant to give players their first exposure to the streets of the Reach before they ever touch a rule.
      Each piece is written to stand alone as flavor, and most double as the origin of a pregen starting character
      players can pick up and run. The fiction is skinned over the canon in world_lore_bible.md (the setting) and
      master_rules_baseline.md (the system) and invents no mechanics; where a story shows a "power," it is a
      lore-facing view of an existing rule.

      How to use it. Hand a table the relevant story cold, with no rules preamble — let them meet the world through a
      person standing in the rain before they meet a stat block. When a story is paired with a pregen, the dossier
      block at the end of the entry gives the Director the character's class, ancestry, ministry/role, and the canon
      threads the story seeds.

          Glossary — Street Cant & Setting Lingo
   New to the Reach? Read this first. The stories that follow are written in the world's own voice — slang, corp
   jargon, and cosmology are dropped in without explanation on purpose, the way a native speaker would. This
   glossary decodes every piece of lingo a first-time player will hit, grouped by what it belongs to. It also covers
   the mechanical vocabulary a playtester needs to run any of the eight pregens — all eight species, all eight
   heroic resources, the Wired (net) subsystems, the Changer/Mutant species subsystems, and the core
   Power-Roll terms are defined below. Definitions are grounded in world_lore_bible.md (the setting) and
   master_rules_baseline.md (the system); where a term maps to a rule, that's noted so the Director can
   connect flavor to mechanics.

   The World & the Hive
    » Veyra ("the Ground") — the prime world itself: "the world that was said, and is still being said." Its people call
      the dirt under their feet "the Ground"; Veyra is the planet. A single willed creation The Light set as its
      proving ground.
    » The hive / the sprawl — the megacity: not a skyline of towers but one titanic vertical megastructure of
      stacked levels rising kilometers into the sky and sinking kilometers underground, built on the ruins of the
      level below it for so long no one alive remembers the ground floor. One enormous stratified organism,
      owned wall-to-wall by the corps.
    » The Reach (Ossian Reach) — the specific hive our stories take place in. ("Reach" is also corp-speak for how
      far a megacorp's power extends — single-hive, multi-hive, or world-spanning — but in the fiction, the
      Reach is home.)
    » Arcology — a self-contained corporate mega-building: housing, offices, security, and life-support sealed
      into one structure. The corp's people live, work, and die without ever leaving it. Krow walked out of a corp
      arcology; the Spires are arcology-penthouses.
    » The overlay — the augmented-reality layer painted over the world through everyone's implants and lenses:
      ad-glass, price-tags on the air, corp-shrine halos, a soft apologetic blue over the puddles "so you wouldn't
      look too hard at what's in them." Reality with a corporate skin. Where the overlay "gets thin," the corp's
      eyes get tired — the deep levels.

   The Vertical — altitude is wealth (top to bottom)
   The hive's one organizing law: the higher you live, the richer you are. You can read a person's whole station
   from the level they sleep on. This ladder is the game's Lifestyle track (Elite→Squatter), which sets recovery,
   safehouse security, and social access.

    » The Crown / the Spires — the sunlit apex: corp arcology-penthouses, executive enclaves, real sky, clean air.
      Obscenely comfortable, endlessly surveilled. Elite Lifestyle. To live here is to be the corp. (Kaïs was a
      patron-house possession in the Spires.)
    » The Upper Tiers — managerial and professional heights: secured condos, warded lofts, still touched by
      daylight. High Lifestyle.
    » The Midlevels / the Grid — the vast wage-working body of the hive: licensed commerce, patrolled corridors,
      the salaryman's stable flat, artificial light standing in for a sky most never see. Order is real but rented —
      everyone one missed payment from falling. Middle Lifestyle. (Kaïs hides here now.)

    » The Underlevels / the Flats — dense, dim, semi-lawless strata where corp authority thins and local power
      fills the gap: gangs, fixers, level-bosses, street-faiths. The edgerunner's natural habitat, where most runs
      begin. Low Lifestyle. (Vessa's corner, Ninth-and-Fold.)
    » The Warrens — the deep, cramped, written-off underlevels — the tangled warren-country of the lower
      Flats and the edge of the Sinks, where the spent, the recycled, and the off-book live. Little law, thin overlay,
      tired cameras. (Krow walked down into the Warrens to disappear.)
    » The Sinks / the Undercity — the deep hive: sunless, half-flooded, built over dead levels and older ruins, all
      but abandoned by the corps above. Little law, little maintenance, little Signal reaching down. Squatter
      Lifestyle and worse — the deep dark where the forgotten live and the buried past stirs. (Vessa's corner sits
      one level above the Sinks.)
    » Down-shaft — a vertical drop between levels; the throat the hive's runoff (and worse) falls down, "going
      somewhere no one likes to think about."
    » → Grace is NOT vertical. Height buys comfort, not virtue — a saint and a demon can share a sublevel.
      Wealth runs up-down; the war runs through every level.

   The Runner Life
    » Ghost — a person the system never recorded: no SIN, no citizenship, no proof you were ever spoken into
      the world at all. To be a ghost is to be free in the only way left — and, in the cosmology, unspoken. The
      game's title namesake.
    » SIN (System Identification Number) — the number every citizen carries: the invisible master-key that opens
      the clinic door, the ration terminal, the transit gate, the whole warm lit world. In the Flats a SIN is "a rumor
      about yourself the Machine can revise at any time." Unverified → one glitch from doesn't exist. A fake SIN
      (rated by quality) is standard runner kit.
    » Edgerunner / runner — the unregistered, deniable operative who works the shadow economy: extraction,
      sabotage, retrieval, muscle. The setting's protagonist archetype — an exception to the corp-engineered
      world where no one has to choose. In GHOSTWIRE canon the classic "shadowrunner" is the Edgerunner /
      Deniable Contractor.
    » Deniable contractor — the legal-fiction job title a runner hides behind; each pregen carries a straight-faced
      cover (Vessa: "pastoral-services contractor"; Krow: "privately-held industrial-security chassis"). The corps'
      shadow war is fought through deniable hands so no seated corp is ever officially at war.
    » Run / the job — a single deniable contract. "You want the next one?"
    » Fixer — the broker who sells introductions, not people: connects clients up-top to runners down-below
      and takes a fee. The runner's lifeline and leash. Mama Cassavir "the Switchboard" is the signature fixer —
      everyone owes her, she writes nothing down, and she guards a crew's anonymity over any client.
    » Renown — your street reputation as a currency: it "opens doors nuyen can't." Fear and results build it;
      charity, as Cassavir needles Vessa, builds nothing — "nobody hires the soft one."
    » Nuyen (¥) — the standard corporate scrip / money of the hive. Foci, gear, and Lifestyle are all priced in
      nuyen.
    » Credstick — a physical money-chip. A certified / anonymous credstick is cold, shadow-ledger money that
      doesn't ask your SIN either — the runner economy's cash.
    » Paydata — stolen or valuable data as a commodity: the thing a run retrieves, sells, or fences. "A piece of
      paydata he could not read and could not sell."

    » The Machine — the runner's name for the whole corporate system: the ledger, the SIN-registry, the overlay,
      the rented order. Vessa's sermons set "the Machine" (gives everything, asks nothing, calls it mercy) against
      "the Light" (asks everything, gives you a choice, calls it grace).

   The Corps & the Powers
    » Megacorp / corp — one of the conglomerates that own, run, and police the hive: corp scrip, corp security,
      corp housing, corp medicine. Above ordinary law and extraterritorial.
    » The Ten Conglomerates — the ten ranking megacorps who hold the seats of power; a seat is the
      government. HALO (sells the sun/light), Ferrum (the ground/industry), Caduceus (medicine/flesh) are
      named in the fiction. The "empty eleventh chair" is the seat that opens only when one of the ten falls.
    » Caduceus — the medical conglomerate; "rents you back your own flesh, dose by dose." The clinic Vessa
      strong-arms is a Caduceus sub-sub-franchise / Grafthouse cast-off.
    » The Radiant Concord — the false corporate church: broadcasts loud, certain, purchased comfort from the
      Cathedral of the Sun and calls it the Light. The photographic negative of real faith. Its Choir of the Silent
      Sun hollows out true street-priests (the "Choirmother" hook). Its word for people like Vessa is lapsed.

   Chrome, Casters & the Cosmology
    » Chrome — cybernetic implants; "the chrome you run on." Powerful, but it erodes the Veil: chrome
      proportionally severs a body from magic (why Vessa and Kaïs run no chrome).
    » Body Integrity — the rules track of how much natural flesh you have left; chrome spends it. At 0 you flatline.
      (Krow, a Full-Conversion Cyborg, is past the spectrum — he runs on frame modules, not implants.)
    » The Veil — the thin membrane between the world and the spirit realms; where it's thin, magic, summoning,
      and the Incursion cross over. "The Veil is thin everywhere, not just in temples." Sense the Veil / Read the
      Weave = reading it.
    » The Word / the Signal — the divine speech that spoke creation into being (The Light) and now runs quiet on
      purpose, because "a thing you're made to love isn't love." Called the Signal in the world's tongue. The three
      ways of touching it are the three class resources.
    » The Light / the Dark (the Machine's mirror) — the cosmic war under the street. The Light acts softly and
      never compels; the Dark "can't make a fire — it can only lean over yours and change what it's for." The
      Incursion is the Dark loose and spreading.
    » The Price — the cost of reaching through the Veil: pacts and dark power write corruption debt into a
      caster. A Light pact (Vessa's) costs little corruption but binds conduct strictly — mercy and the protection
      of innocents.
    » The Wired — the deep-net / cyber-net woven through every level; "what you jack into," and the thinnest
      place of all, where the worst AIs may be Edge-demons wearing a machine.

   The Castes (engineered peoples)
   The corps gene-forged human lines a thousand years ago and assigned them stations — the class system
   with the serial numbers filed off.

    » Corran ("squats," Homo sapiens fossor) — the labor caste: built compact, immensely strong, and hardy to
      crawl the deep mines and cramped service-crawls no baseline human could survive. Unyielding — the
      "immovable at a doorway." (Vessa.)




GHOSTWIRE // deniable-contractor field dossiers                                                                        11
    » Elvani ("Elf," Aesthetic-Class / Highborn) — the beauty caste: ageless, alluring, engineered aristocracy, made
      to be owned as living status symbols. Grant no innate magic — decorative by design. A "corp-doll" is the
      street slur. (Kaïs.)
    » Cyborg (Full-Conversion, Machina sapiens) — a person whose body is entirely machine: heals only by tech,
      can never use magic (Arcane Severance), and at 0 Stamina enters System Crisis instead of dying quietly. A
      corp-built Warframe is a combat-chassis Cyborg. (Krow, serial KRV-9.)
    » Goliar ("Ogre," Homo sapiens giganticus) — the heavy caste: towering, crushing engineered muscle bred for
      hazardous industry, demolition, and enforcement — the brute counterpart to the Corran's skilled labor. The
      engineered heavy: towering, crushing, and impossible to ignore. Their Intimidating Bulk turns sheer mass
      into fear-based social power. "Ogre" is the slur. (Barak, nine feet of grey demolition plate.)
    » Changer ("were," Homo sapiens versipellis, "skin-turner") — the metamorphic caste: shapeshifters with
      three forms — a human-passing base, an overtly bestial Hybrid Form (claws, fur/fangs, upright), and a fully
      animal Beast Form. Shifting is fast and cheap by design — fluid transformation, not a once-a-day ultimate.

                                   The Lamp on Ninth
                                    A GHOSTWIRE story · the Flats, first light

   The rain in the Flats never fell so much as leaked — a warm, chemical drizzle wrung out of the ceiling
   four levels up, where the Grid's runoff got tired of being someone else's problem and started being
   yours. Vessa Corran-Dov had stopped hearing it years ago, the way you stop hearing your own
   heartbeat. It ticked on the tin awning over her door, it beaded on the dead ad-glass across the alley,
   and it ran in a thin grey rope down the throat of the down-shaft at the end of the block, going
   somewhere no one on Ninth liked to think about.

   She was already awake. She was always already awake.

   Vessa was Corran — built low and wide and unbudgeable, the way the old corps had made her people a
   thousand years back to crawl the mine-throats and the crush-narrow service crawls where a taller
   body would have died folded in half. Homo sapiens fossor, the corp gene-registries still called her line,
   in the small print no one read. Squat, the Grid called her, when it bothered to call her anything. She had
   a stonemason's forearms and a face like a closed fist, and she wore the grey wool of no church anyone
   had licensed, because the only church that would have licensed her was the one she'd spent her life
   refusing.

   She lit the lamp first. Always the lamp first.

   It sat in the window of the storefront that used to sell filtration cartridges before the filtration
   company got bought, and got bought again, and finally stopped existing in the way corps stopped
   existing — not with a closure but with a silence. Now the storefront was a soup line four mornings a
   week and a place people came when they were out of every other place. The lamp was real fire. That
   was the whole point of it. Everything else on Ninth glowed — the overlay painted halos on the corp
   shrines and price-tags on the air and a soft, apologetic blue over the puddles so you wouldn't look too
   hard at what was in them — but the lamp was a wick and a cup of rendered fat, and it did not ask your
   SIN for permission to be warm.

   She thumbed it alight and said the small words she always said, not loud. Let the ones who need it find
   the door.

   Somewhere a long way above her, the Word had spoken the whole world into being and then, by every
   account she believed, gone quiet on purpose — because a thing you're made to love isn't love, and the
   Light would rather be doubted than obeyed. Vessa found that easy to believe. She'd been made to
   work. She knew exactly what it did to a thing, being made for a purpose. The miracle wasn't that the
   Light shouted. The miracle was that it didn't, and people chose it anyway, one cold soul at a time, on
   corners like this one.

   Her Conviction was low this morning. She could feel it the way you feel a battery going — not empty,
   just thin, the quiet drip of faith that filled her back up overnight not quite keeping pace with the
   drawing-down. It had been a hard week. It was always a hard week. That was the arithmetic of the thing
   nobody told you when you took up the pact: the Light gives, and the Light asks, and the asking is the
   part that keeps you honest.


   The first one through the door was Tomas.

   He was eleven, maybe twelve, human, all elbows, with the specific grey pallor of a kid who ate when
   there was eating. He came in sideways out of the drizzle and stood dripping on her floor and would not
   look at her, which meant he wanted something and was ashamed of it, which meant it was about his
   mother.

   "She won't wake up right," he said to the floor.

   Vessa was already reaching for her coat. "Won't, or can't?"

   "The card." He finally looked up, and his eyes were the worst thing she'd see all day, because they were
   hoping, and hope on a Flats kid's face was a wound. "The clinic reader wouldn't take her card. It said — it
   said unverified. She's got the cough, the wet one, and they wouldn't scan her past the door 'cause the
   card said unverified and I don't—"

   "I know what unverified means." She did. It meant his mother's SIN — the number every citizen carried,
   the invisible key that opened the clinic door and the ration terminal and the transit gate and the whole
   warm lit world — had hiccupped, or lapsed, or been quietly flagged by some system three owners
   removed from anyone who could be asked why. In the Flats a SIN was a rumor about yourself that the
   Machine could revise at any time. Half of Ninth lived one glitch away from unverified, which was one
   glitch away from doesn't exist, which — if you were poor and sick and eleven years old on a Thursday —
   was one glitch away from dead on a clinic step in the warm rain.

   "I don't have card money," Tomas said. "I don't have—"

   "I didn't ask you for money." She got the door. "Walk fast. My legs are short."


   She did not heal his mother. She wanted that understood, in her own head, always — she was not a
   machine you fed a prayer and got a cure. What she did was walk to the clinic on the Ninth-and-Fold
   corner, plant her unbudgeable Corran body in the doorway the reader wouldn't let the woman through,
   and not move.

   "Ma'am." The clinic wasn't a corp clinic — it was a Caduceus sub-sub-franchise, a Grafthouse cast-off,
   one bored tech and a diagnostic arch and a policy engine that answered to a spreadsheet in a tower
   she'd never see. "The reader flagged her. I can't scan an unverified. It's not me, it's the—"

   "System. I know." Vessa put a hand flat on the arch, gentle, the way you'd touch a frightened animal. She
   let a little of the drip go — not a spell you could see, nothing that would trip a sensor, just the warmth
   she carried banking up in her chest and going out, into the room, into the tech, into the tired air. Sense
   the Veil, the old ministries called it; she just called it paying attention. The tech was nineteen. The tech
   had been yelled at four times before breakfast. The tech was not the enemy. The Machine was very
   good at making the tired yell at the poor so the powerful never had to.

   "Her name is Aline," Vessa said. "She's got two kids and a wet cough and she's standing in your rain
   because a number blinked. You didn't make the rule. I'm not asking you to break it." She reached into
   her coat and set a certified credstick on the counter — anonymous, cold, shadow-ledger money, the
   kind that didn't ask your SIN either. Payday from a job she didn't discuss. "I'm asking you to run her as a
   walk-in cash patient. No card. No scan. Your policy engine allows it. It just doesn't advertise it."

   The tech looked at the stick. Looked at the small, immovable woman in unlicensed grey. Looked, for one
   second, at the boy.

   "...Cash walk-in," he said, and turned the arch's reader off, and turned the diagnostic on. "Bring her
   through."

   It cost Vessa forty percent of the run money and a favor she'd now owe the fixer who'd fronted the
   credstick. It bought a nebulizer course and a scrip and a woman who would wake up right tomorrow.
   The Light did not descend. Nobody's hands glowed. That was the shape of real faith on Ninth: it looked,
   from the outside, almost exactly like a stubborn person spending money she didn't have on a stranger,
   and refusing to move.

   Which, Vessa thought, was probably the point of that too.


   Mama Cassavir found her on the walk back, which meant Cassavir had wanted to be found.

   You didn't reach the Switchboard; the Switchboard reached you, through a door you didn't know was a
   comm, in a voice that came up out of a dead ad-panel as you passed. "Preacher." The panel's broken
   glass flickered with the shape of a face and then thought better of it. "You spent my stick."

   "I spent your stick." No use pretending. Cassavir knew where every favor on three levels slept at night.
   "A woman and a wet cough."

   "Charity." The word came dry as dust. Cassavir ran the Flats the way a heart runs a body — everyone
   owed her, everyone needed her, and she kept the whole tangle of debt and rumor and grudge in a head
   that never wrote anything down. "You know what charity does to your Renown, Vessa? Nothing.
   Nobody's scared of the woman who gives it away. Nobody hires the soft one."

   "Nobody trusts the hard one with their kids, either." Vessa kept walking; the panel's voice paced her,
   jumping panel to dead panel down the block. "You reached me for a reason. It wasn't to scold my
   accounting."

   A pause. Even the rain seemed to hold it.

   "There's a woman working the Fold-side blocks," Cassavir said, and something in the dryness had gone
   careful. "Calls herself the Choirmother. Feeds people. Real food, good food, more than you can afford,
   Preacher, a lot more than you can afford. Wards their kids. Prays over the sick and — here's the part —
   the sick get better. People are moving to her corners. Yours are getting thin."

   Vessa stopped.

   "You want me to tell you she's a fraud," Cassavir went on. "She's not. That's the trouble. I looked. Her
   faith reads true. She started like you — nothing, a lamp, a soup line. But somebody found her.
   Somebody with resources. There's a soft-voiced fella comes by from up the Spire, calls himself her
   mentor, and ever since he started coming 'round, her food got better and her sermons got — smoother.
   Comforting. The kind that doesn't ask anything of anybody." The panel's flicker leaned closer. "You know
   that smell, Preacher. The kind of church that gives you everything and asks you for nothing. You've
   spent your whole life not being that church."

   Vessa knew the smell. It was the smell of the Cathedral of the Sun, four hundred levels up, where the
   Radiant Concord broadcast comfort to a billion souls and called it the Light — the loud, certain,
   purchased faith that was the exact photographic negative of everything the real Light did. The church
   that had a name for people like Vessa. The name was lapsed.

   "She doesn't know," Vessa said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the pattern before, in smaller
   ways: a good soul, a real fire, and then a patient hand from above that fed the fire the wrong fuel one
   degree at a time, until one morning it was still burning and it was warming the wrong thing, and the
   woman holding the match would swear on her life it was the same flame. The Dark can't make a fire, her
   own pact-work had taught her. It can only lean over yours and change what it's for.

   "No," Cassavir agreed. "She doesn't know. She thinks it's answered prayer. Might be the saddest thing
   on the level." The dry voice sharpened to its point, the reason the Switchboard had reached her at all.
   "Somebody's gonna have to walk onto the Fold-side and find out how far gone she is. Somebody who
   can tell a true fire from a counterfeit, up close, without getting warmed stupid by it. That somebody's
   got a lamp and short legs and forty percent of my credstick left to her name."

   The rain ticked on the tin. Down the block, Vessa's own storefront glowed its one honest un-networked
   orange, small against the whole apologetic blue overlay of a world designed so no one had to look too
   hard at what was in the puddles.

   Somewhere far below the down-shaft, in the dark the overlay didn't reach, something that had been a
   foundation once and was now only quiet listened to the rain come down.

   "Tell the fixer I'm listening," Vessa said. "And tell her the charity stands. The woman and the cough.
   That's not for sale."

   A GHOSTWIRE story — the Grid, Ossian Reach

   There is a particular sound a corp gala makes at the end of the night, when the guests have gone and the
   caterers are stacking the good glass: a high, exhausted ringing, crystal touching crystal, the last note of
   money at rest. Kaïs Vahn-Estal had spent nineteen years learning to stand inside that sound and smile. He
   could still hear it, some nights, three levels down and a whole life away, over the rain hammering the polymer
   roof of a Grid tenement that had never once held a party.

   He was, by design, beautiful. Not handsome — beautiful the way a blade is, the way a bill of sale is: on purpose,
   at cost, for someone. The corp that had commissioned his line called it Aesthetic-Class. The salons called it
   Highborn. The people in this stairwell, if they knew what he was, would call him a corp-doll and spit, and they
   would not be wrong. He had been a fixture in a patron-house in the Spires — a beautiful thing owned by a
   beautiful family, brought out to make deals warmer and rivals careless, and put back in his rooms when the
   lights came up. Ageless, they'd promised him. You will be lovely when everyone who bought you is dead.

   He had believed, for a long time, that this was the best a thing like him could hope for.

   The storm changed that. Not a metaphor — an actual storm, the kind that only happens up high where the
   hive's climate stacks fail: a pressure front rolling through the Spires' open galleries, and Kaïs standing at a
   window he was not supposed to touch, feeling the charge build in the air like a held breath. He had never
   been taught what he was, underneath the engineering. No one teaches the furniture that it can burn. But the
   charge answered him the way a dog answers a whistle it has been waiting its whole life to hear, and when the
   patron's daughter put her hand on the back of his neck the way she always did, the window behind him did
   not shatter inward.

   It shattered out.

   They did not call it magic, in the house. They called it a malfunction, and then a liability, and then they called
   the people who erase liabilities. Kaïs did not wait to be erased. He took the storm down the hive with him, one
   level at a time, learning on the way what the salons had spent centuries making sure his kind never learned:
   that the Veil is thin everywhere, not just in temples, and that a mind trained to read a room can be trained to
   read the charge in the air, the water in a pipe, the current in a wall. That beauty was not the only thing they
   had accidentally built into him.

   Now he paid a Corran fixer named Cassavir for the privilege of being no one.

   The job was simple, which meant it was not. A mid-Grid data-broker had bought a piece of paydata he could
   not read and could not sell, and it was killing him — the seller had come back wanting it returned, and the
   seller had brought friends. Cassavir, who took clean contracts from above and dirty ones from below and
   connected the two for a fee, had offered Kaïs the retrieval because it needed someone who could walk into a
   corporate stratum and be assumed to belong there. You have the face for it, the old woman had said, not
   unkindly, over the hum of a dead switching-station wired back to life. Use the thing they built you for. Spite is
   a fuel like any other.

   So Kaïs stood now in a Grid corridor that smelled of ozone and cheap noodles, wearing a coat that cost more
   than the corridor, watching four men take apart a data-broker's flat with the patience of people who have
   done this before and expect to be paid either way. He could have talked his way past them. That was the old



GHOSTWIRE // deniable-contractor field dossiers                                                                         26
   tool, the built-in one, the one the house had sharpened. He was very good at it. He hated it.

   He reached for the other thing instead.

   It came the way it always came now — not as a bolt but as a gathering. He stood still and let himself feel the
   storm that a city always is: the current running in the walls, the water sitting in the sprinkler main above the
   drop-ceiling, the charge he could pull out of the very damp of the air. He held it the way he had once held a
   smile through a four-hour dinner, patiently, letting it build, letting it want, and the men did not notice him at
   all, because a thing like him is scenery until it is not.

   The first he gave the water. The sprinkler main above the doorway did not so much break as decide to be
   somewhere else, all at once, and he went down under it choking and blind. The second and third he gave the
   storm proper — he opened his hand and the charge he'd been holding went to ground through the nearest
   thing that would carry it, which was the two men standing on a wet floor in conductive boots, and the
   corridor lit blue-white and the noodle-smell became a burning smell and they folded like the puppets the
   house had always said he was.

   The fourth ran. He let him. He was not, whatever the house had made him, in the business of killing people who
   had stopped being a problem.

   The data-broker was under his own overturned table, holding the piece of paydata like it might warm him. He
   crouched to his level — a courtesy, not a threat — and for a moment the old tool and the new one were the
   same thing: the face they built to make you trust it, over the storm they didn't know they'd built underneath.

   "You're going to give that to me," he said, "and I'm going to give it back to the people it belongs to, and you're
   going to go somewhere the seller's friends can't count. That's the whole deal. There's no better one in this."

   He gave it to him. They almost always did. It was the one part of the house's training he had not learned to
   hate, because down here he got to choose what he used it for.

   Later, on the wet stairs between the Grid and the Flats, he stopped where the corp lighting gave out and the
   local stuff took over — the seam where up became down. Rain found its way even here, three levels below
   where rain should be, running down through the hive's wounded plumbing to fall on people who had never
   seen a sky. He held his hand out into it and let a small charge dance between his fingers, blue and quiet, just
   to know it was his.

   Somewhere above him, in a Spires gallery with real windows and imported weather, a patron-house was still
   pretending it had merely lost a favored possession, and not that its favored possession had walked down into
   the body of the hive and learned to call the lightning. They would come looking eventually. Ageless cut both
   ways: he would still be here, still lovely, still furious, long after the daughter who'd put her hand on his neck
   was dust — and he intended to use every year of it.

   Cassavir's voice crackled in his cheap earpiece, dry as ever. "You're on the news, Highborn. 'Freak power
   surge, Grid level forty-one.' Very tidy. You want the next one, or you want to go be beautiful and quiet for a
   while?"

   Kaïs closed his hand. The charge went out. The rain kept coming down through the broken hive, the way it
   always had, the way it always would, indifferent to who owned the roof.

   "The next one," he said.

   The corp had a word for what was about to happen to him, and the word was decommission. It was a
   clean word. It was the word you used for a satellite you let fall, for a building you let the sky take back,
   for a debt you let quietly lapse. It was not the word you used for killing a person, because you did not
   use any word for that at all when the thing in question had a serial number where its name should be.

   His serial number was KRV-9. Nine. The Warrens called him Krow, because the Warrens could not be
   bothered with the rest of it, and because he was tall and black-plated and stood on the corners where
   the carrion was, and the name fit the way a coat fits after you've worn it long enough to forget it was
   ever a gift.

   He had been a good asset. That was not pride — Krow was not entirely sure he still had the hardware

   contracts for the company that built him. Breaching, holding, ending. He had never glitched. He had

   company's accountants finally ran his numbers — upkeep, chassis fatigue, the cost of a newer line of

   Decommission KRV-9. Reclaim salvageable modules. Recycle chassis.

   He'd read the order himself. That was the joke of it, if a joke was a thing he could still hold. They'd never
   bothered to firewall him out of his own maintenance queue, because why would an asset read its own

                          A GHOSTWIRE story · the Flats, a shuttered foundry-floor

   The kid across the table was doing the math, and Barak let him.

   It was good math to let a man do. The kid — Sover, a mid-tier fixer's runner with a jawline that had
   never been broken and a jacket that cost more than the room they sat in — had come to the shuttered
   foundry to renegotiate. That was the word he'd used on the comm. Renegotiate. He'd come with two
   shooters and a smile and the confidence of a man who had read Barak's file and seen the word Goliar
   and, under it, the word grunt, and had decided the second word was the one that counted.

   He was doing the math now because Barak had come out of the dark of the foundry floor and stood up
   to his full height, and the math had changed.

   Nine feet of engineered muscle was the first line of the equation. That was just breeding — the corps
   had grown Barak in a tank on the Kwan-Yang heavy line to lift what forklifts couldn't and break what
   charges wouldn't, and he'd have been a wall of a man on flesh alone. But the corps hadn't stopped at
   flesh. They never did, with the muscle. They'd laced his bones with reinforced weave and plated his
   torso in dermal armor and hung a hydraulic ram where his right forearm used to end, because a
   security Goliar that could also breach was worth two line items, and the accountants liked a
   two-for-one.

   So the second line of the kid's math was the chrome. It caught the work-light in long dull grey planes
   across Barak's chest and shoulders, and it did not gleam prettily the way a Face's soft social suite
   gleamed. It looked like what it was: industrial demolition hardware, worn on the outside of a person, by
   choice.

   That was the part the kid couldn't make balance. Barak watched him try. He's a Face, the file had said. A
   fixer. A talker. And every talker the kid had ever met kept the chrome hidden, or wore none at all,
   because everyone knew that visible metal cost you the room — it made people wary, it made them
   cold, it ate the warmth a smooth operator ran on. A fixer wrapped in demolition plate was a
   contradiction. It was a man who had thrown away the very thing his trade was built on.

   Except.

   The corp file had said he couldn't charm. Minus one Presence, the geneticists wrote, blunt in polite
   company — their clean way of saying the tank had made a weapon, not a diplomat. And the file, as the
   file always did, had gotten him exactly wrong. Because a big man who is smart — and Barak was smart
   — learns very early that the most disarming thing in the world is a large and obviously dangerous thing
   being gentle. His voice had come out of the vat warm. Warm and low and unhurried, a voice you
   wanted to lean toward, a voice that made a frightened man feel, against all the evidence of his eyes,
   that everything was going to be fine. Barak had learned to use the warmth the way another man used a



GHOSTWIRE // deniable-contractor field dossiers                                                                43
   smile — to open the door — and he had learned the other thing too, the thing that lived in the oldest
   part of the brain, below language, in the place that knows the difference between a large thing and a
   small one. He had learned that the warmth was only worth what the roar made it worth.

   He had leaned into the thing he was. He had spent his own hard nuyen, after he walked off the corp
   leash, buying more of the chrome, not less. Because on him — nine feet, size Large, engineered to loom
   — the metal did not read as cold. It read as inevitable. Every plate said the same plain sentence the
   accountants had said about the crew that came to collect his salvage, once: this ends one way.

   "You came to renegotiate," Barak said, and his voice was the warm one, the comforting one, the
   big-man-being-kind one, and it filled the foundry the way warm water fills a boot. He might have been
   welcoming the kid to a family dinner. "Sit. Say the new terms. Nobody here is in a hurry."

   counting his own guns. Barak let him count. He kept the warmth on his face the whole time, patient and
   kind, a host waiting for a nervous guest to get comfortable — and that was somehow worse than a

   Then the shooter on the left let his hand drift toward his coat.

   Barak did not move fast. He simply stopped being warm. — "HAND." The single word came up out of him
   and hit the foundry like a dropped girder, the vocal modulator throwing it off the dead machines until it
   seemed to come from every direction at once, a wall of sound with the whole nine feet of him behind it,
   and the shooter's hand froze as though the air had gone solid around his wrist. For one full second the
   roar hung in the room and every man in it remembered, all at once and all the way down, exactly what
   they were standing next to. Then, into that ringing silence, Barak set his chromed right forearm — the
   ram, the breaching hardware, the thing built to open bank-vault doors — flat on the steel table, gently,
   and the table took his weight with a groan of protest, and did not break, and every man in the room
   understood in his spine that the not breaking was a choice Barak had made about the table and was, at
   this exact moment, also making about them.

   "The terms," Barak said. And just like that the warmth was back, soft and unhurried, as if the roar had
   been someone else entirely — and the whiplash of it, the kindness returning to the same throat that
   had just shaken the room, was the most frightening thing the kid had felt all night.

   And here was the trick the fixer's file would never capture, because it wasn't in any file — it was in him,
   the second half of the thing the corps threw away. Barak stayed in the warm voice now, and let it go
   warmer still, past reassurance, into something that sounded, impossibly, like care. Because the roar
   was the door, but he had learned — slow, the way he learned everything, and therefore permanently —
   that fear only opened the room. It did not close a deal. What closed a deal was the warm voice coming
   back afterward, the offer you made a frightened man to let him walk out whole, delivered in the same
   gentle rumble that had, thirty seconds ago, nearly stopped his heart. The thing about being the biggest,
   most obviously lethal object in any room was that you could afford to be generous in a way a smaller,



GHOSTWIRE // deniable-contractor field dossiers                                                                  44
   more nervous operator never could — and your generosity landed all the harder for what it followed.

   "You keep your original percentage," Barak said, to the frightened kid. "You keep your shooters. You
   keep your teeth. And you go back to whoever sent you and you tell them the foundry crew honors its
   contracts, and expects the same, and does not renegotiate — but does not hold a grudge, either, for a
   man doing his boss's errand." A pause, weighted. "That is a better deal than the one you walked in here
   to make. You just could not see it, over the chrome."

   Sover looked at him for a long moment. Then — slowly, the way you move around a large animal you
   have decided is not, today, going to eat you — he nodded. And he took the deal, which was the old deal,
   which was the deal Barak had wanted all along.

   They left. Nobody's teeth stayed on the foundry floor. That was, Barak reflected, standing alone again in
   the dark among the cold dead machines that had been built to do exactly what the corps had built him
   to do, the entire point.

   An angry Goliar coming through the wall ended most negotiations early. Barak had learned to be the
   wall that didn't come through — the wall that could, and chose, visibly, expensively, in front of everyone,
   not to. That choice was worth more than any argument. He wore it in grey plate across his chest so no
   one ever had to wonder whether he had it. But the plate only made men wary. It was the voice that
   made them loyal — the warm rumble that welcomed them in, the roar that reminded them why they
   should not test it, and the warmth returning afterward, every time, like a promise kept. Men followed
   the plate out of fear. They followed the voice because some frightened animal part of them had
   decided, in the whiplash between the roar and the kindness, that this enormous grey thing had chosen
   to be gentle with them, and might not always.

   The Corp had built him to break things and be pointed. He had kept the being-hard-to-argue-with. He
   had thrown away the being-pointed.

   He ran his own board now. And when the crew sat down to a deal, Barak sat where everyone could see
   him, and spoke warmly, and welcomed them in — and let them all carry, somewhere behind the ear, the
   memory of what the warm voice sounded like when it stopped being warm.

   It was, the others had learned, the gentlest voice in the Reach. Right up until it wasn't.

                                       The Long Sight
                 A GHOSTWIRE story · the roofline above the Grid, an hour before the meet

   From up here the Hive looked almost honest.

   Wren lay flat on the cold ceramic lip of a Grid-tier maintenance spar, four hundred meters above the
   street she'd been paid to watch, and let the wind do what it wanted with her coat. She'd found the
   perch an hour ago the way she found all her perches — she'd gone up as a bird, circled once on the
   thermal that always rose off the coolant stacks at dusk, and picked the ledge with the cleanest
   sightline and the worst approach. The worst approach for anyone coming at her. The best approach
   was always straight down, and straight down was hers alone.

   That was the thing the ground people never understood about a Wereraven. They thought the gift was
   the flying. It wasn't. Anyone with a rotor-drone and a spare afternoon could get height. The gift was the
   eye — the long, patient, raptor-tuned eye the change had left even in her human face, the eye that
   could read a man's shoulders from four hundred meters and know, before he knew it himself, that he
   was about to reach for a gun.

   She was reading shoulders now.

   The meet was set for a plaza three tiers down, a false-marble apron outside a corp-front noodle house
   that the fixer had sworn was neutral ground. Wren had stopped believing in neutral ground the first
   time she'd watched a neutral ground fill up with shooters from a hundred meters up while the marks
   below smiled and shook hands and had no idea. Neutral ground was a story the ground told itself. From
   the air there was no such thing — only the ground you could see all of, and the ground you couldn't.

   She could see all of this one.

   Her crew's client — a nervous Grid-tier data-broker with a briefcase and a bad haircut — was already
   in position at a café table, doing the small brave things frightened people do to look calm. Two tables
   over: a couple, too still, not talking, coffees untouched. Corp muscle, plainclothes. Wren had marked
   them the moment they sat. Marked — she felt the little internal click of it, the study-and-tag her whole
   trade turned on, the way her attention seized a target and did not let go. She knew the woman's lowest
   guard now the way she knew the wind. She could put a round through the gap in the man's cheap
   armor from here, in this wind, and she would not miss, because missing was for people who guessed,
   and Wren did not guess. Wren watched, and watching, done long enough and patiently enough, stops
   being a guess and becomes a fact.

   But she wasn't here to shoot. She was here to see, and to tell her crew what she saw, half a second
   before it mattered, which in her experience was the whole of the difference between a job and a
   funeral.

   She keyed her throat-mic. "Two watchers, plainclothes, the couple at your eight," she said, low. "Corp,
   not street — they've got the posture. They're not here for your client. They're here for the briefcase,
   which means they already know what's in it, which means your fixer talked." A pause while her long eye
   swept the plaza edge, the rooflines, the stairwell mouths — the reflex of a creature that has spent its
   life being small prey to bigger things and has decided, permanently, never to be surprised again. "Third
   one. Stairwell northeast, hasn't come out yet. He's the closer. When the couple stands, he moves. That's
   your tell. You've got about nine seconds after they stand."

   "Copy," her crew lead breathed. "Can you take the closer?"

   Wren considered the stairwell mouth, the angle, the wind, the drop.

   "I can do better than take him," she said. "I can make sure nobody has to."

   Because here was the second thing the ground people didn't understand, the thing even her own crew
   sometimes forgot: the Wereraven's real weapon wasn't the rifle laid cold and quiet along the ledge
   beside her. It was the warning. A shooter who knows he's seen is a shooter doing math instead of
   shooting. Wren had ended more fights by being visibly, undeniably watching — a single glint of a scope
   from an impossible height, a shadow with wings crossing the light at the wrong moment — than she
   had ever ended with the trigger. The corp closer in the stairwell was about to learn that the plaza had a
   ceiling, and that the ceiling had an eye, and that the eye had already found him.

   She let the last of the dusk-light catch her scope, once, deliberately, angled down into the stairwell
   dark. A flat silver wink. I see you.

   In the stairwell, the third man went very still.

   The couple did not stand. The briefcase changed hands. Wren's nervous little client walked out of the
   plaza on his own two legs with his bad haircut and his data intact, and never once looked up, and never
   knew that the reason he was alive was lying flat on a cold ledge four hundred meters over his head,
   watching, the way she was always watching, the way the long sight had taught her she would always
   have to.

   When it was done she didn't take the stairs. Scouts who take the stairs get met on the stairs.

   She rolled off the ledge into the dark and the drop, and somewhere in the first half-second of falling
   the change took her — bones going hollow and light, the coat becoming wings, the long human sight
   folding down into the older, sharper thing it had grown out of — and Wren Sable-Corvin caught the
   coolant thermal on a black spread of feathers and climbed, unhurried, back up into the only tier of the
   Hive that was ever really hers.

   The high one. The one you could see all of.

                          A GHOSTWIRE story · a coffin-motel cell in the Grid, 03:00

   The dead don't sleep, which is how Sabbat knew it was three in the morning without a clock.

   He lay in the coffin-motel cell the way he always lay — flat on his back on the rented slab, hands folded
   on his chest like something in a mortuary, glowing eyes open to the low ceiling twelve centimeters
   above his face. He didn't need the sleep. He didn't need the air the cell's cheap scrubber pushed at him
   either, or the nutrient paste in the dispenser, or any of the small living things the other renters paid for.
   Deathless Physiology, a corp coroner had called it, back when he'd had a corp and a name and a pulse.
   Now he just called it quiet. The dead were very quiet. It was the living who could never shut up.

   Except tonight the dead were talking.

   He'd felt it come on an hour ago — the itch behind the glowing eyes, the pull he'd learned not to fight,
   the sense of a frequency just below hearing where the net's ghosts drifted. Because here was the thing
   no coroner had put in the file, the thing Sabbat had discovered the first time he rose from an alley
   death with his skull full of static: the net had ghosts too. Not metaphors. Not programs. Ghosts —
   fragments of dead code, orphaned processes, the digital dead who'd been cut loose when their
   systems were decommissioned and had simply refused, like him, to stop. Most deckers couldn't hear
   them. Most mages couldn't touch them. But a dead man who'd learned to speak to the recently
   deceased could, it turned out, speak to the deceased of the machine world just as easily. They were
   the same congregation. They were his.

   He reached up — not with his hands, which stayed folded on his chest — and touched the frequency,
   and the frequency answered.

   "Compile," Sabbat murmured, and out of the dark of the cell a shape unfolded: a Data-sprite, a scrap of
   half-aware code shaped, by his expectation, like a small pale bird made of drifting numbers. It hung in
   the air over his coffin-slab and turned its non-face toward him and waited, the way the recently dead
   always waited on him — patient, incurious, glad of the attention. He'd never had to force a sprite. Other
   technomancers wrestled the net for its spirits, bribed them, bound them screaming. Sabbat's came
   willingly. Death recognized death. The ghosts of the machine world looked at the cold glowing-eyed
   thing on the rented slab and saw, correctly, one of their own.

   "There's a man outside my door," Sabbat told the sprite, in the flat unhurried voice of someone who had
   stopped being afraid of endings a long time ago. "Been there four minutes. Go and see whose he is."

   The Data-sprite folded itself thin and slid under the coffin-cell's door into the corridor, and for a
   moment Sabbat rode its borrowed senses out into the hall — Deep Communion, the mages would call
   it, though he needed no rig and no deck to do it; the communion was the interface, the way talking to a
   ghost was the interface for a séance. Through the sprite's eyes he saw the man: cheap armor under a



GHOSTWIRE // deniable-contractor field dossiers                                                                    62
   courier's jacket, a fresh corporate SIN, and slaved to his belt a little killware drone still folded up asleep.
   Not a courier. A cleaner. Someone had finally decided that the dead man who talked to dead code
   knew too much, and had sent a living man to make him properly dead.

   Sabbat almost smiled. It was a common mistake. People saw the rot and the glowing eyes and thought
   dead meant fragile. They had it exactly backwards.

   He compiled the second sprite without rising from the slab — an Attack-sprite this time, a knot of
   hostile code shaped like a coiled black thing — and then a third, because the Weaver's gift was the
   congregation, the many, and Sabbat was never more himself than when the ghosts crowded thick
   around him. Three sprites now, drifting in the dark of a coffin motel, and a fourth compiling, the
   frequency deepening with each one until the whole cell hummed with the presence of the willing dead.

   Out in the corridor the cleaner keyed a lockpick-jack into Sabbat's door.

   Sabbat sent the congregation through the wall to meet him — not himself, he stayed folded on his slab,
   hands on his chest, glowing eyes on the ceiling — just the ghosts, his ghosts, the ones who came when
   he called because they knew him for kin. The Attack-sprite jacked the cleaner's own killware drone and
   turned it. The Data-sprite blinded his SIN-reader and his optics at once. The Ward-sprite wrapped
   Sabbat's cell in a skin of resonance so that even if the man got the door open he'd find nothing his
   weapons understood how to hurt.

   It was over in the time it takes a living heart to beat a dozen times. The cleaner fled — babbling, later, in
   whatever debrief his corp gave him, about a coffin cell full of ghosts, about a dead thing that hadn't
   even sat up, about numbers in the dark that moved like birds. Nobody believed him. Nobody ever did.
   That, too, was Sabbat's protection: he was impossible, and impossible things don't get budgeted for.

   When the corridor was empty again Sabbat let the congregation fade — the sprites decompiling one
   by one, dissolving back into the frequency they'd risen from, going home to the quiet the way he would,
   someday, when his own unfinished business finally let him rest. He did not know yet what that business
   was. He'd died in an alley with his purpose already half-erased; recovering it was the long work of his
   second life. But he knew this much: the dead were patient, and he had all the time that not-dying gave
   him, and a congregation of ghosts who came when he called.

   He folded his hands back on his chest. He fixed his glowing eyes on the ceiling twelve centimeters
   above his face.

   And in the quiet that only the dead really know, Sabbat listened to the frequency below hearing, where

   over any client. Vira had taken the stick, and gone back down where she belonged.

   Vira Kellis-Nade lay flat in the crawlspace with her cheek against cold ferroconcrete, and the
   Warren-quiet hummed in her back teeth — the low, wet drone of a level that the corps had written off
   and the overlay had stopped painting years ago. Down here the AR went thin. Down here the cameras
   were tired. Down here she was home.

   Getting into the gallery had been the easy part, and she'd done it the way her line always did — not
   through a door. Two levels up there was a service-duct grille too tight for any grown thing to pass,
   which was exactly why she'd chosen it. She'd crouched in front of it in the dark, breathed out, and let
   the change take her — a fast, fluid thing, no ritual to it, just a shrug of will and a maneuver's worth of
   time: her spine shortened, her clothes and her drone-case webbing folding away into fur and the drop
   of her own weight, until what pressed up to the grille was not a woman but a long grey rat with a tech's
   clever eyes. Beast Form. She'd oozed through a gap a fist couldn't fit, dropped four feet into the duct
   on the far side, and scurried — silent on the filth-slick metal, sure-footed where a human would have
   clanged, reading the black by smell and the low animal light of her own eyes. She'd come out into the
   dead gallery, found her crawlspace, and only then breathed back up into her own shape, the case
   reassembling around her as she rose. No door had opened. No sensor had blinked. The Warren had
   simply swallowed a rat and coughed up an operator.

   Nine ways out. She had mapped them on the way in, the way her line always did — the grille she'd come
   through as a rat, the gnaw-hole she'd widened last month with a cutting torch, the down-shaft she
   could drop into and never touch a wall, six more. Nine. She never worked a room with fewer than three,
   and she was proudest of the nights she used none of them.

   "Contact," Cassavir said in her ear, dry as a blown fuse. "Two shooters, coming your gallery. They know
   you're down here, sweet. They don't know where."

   "They never do," Vira said, and opened the case.

   It wasn't a weapon. People always expected a weapon — a rat-thing in a crawlspace, they pictured
   teeth. What Vira Kellis-Nade carried was a hardshell flight case the size of a footlocker, and when the
   lid came up it exhaled a soft blue glow and the smell of warm electronics, and the swarm woke up.

   Four of them. Palm-sized quadrotors, matte and scuffed, each one a machine she'd built with her own
   hands from grey-market parts and salvage and spite. She didn't deploy them so much as let them go —
   a flick of will down the control rig seated behind her ear, and they lifted out of the case one after
   another, spun up, and fanned into the dark gallery like a breath held too long finally let out.

   She felt the room bloom open in her head. Not her eyes — theirs. Four sensor-feeds stitched into one
   wide picture behind her forehead: the two shooters edging up the gallery in a covering stagger, the
   heat of them, the cheap corp-issue smartguns, the way the near one kept glancing at the ducts
   because someone had warned him about the ducts.

   Good. Let him watch the ducts. The ducts were where she wasn't.

   "Spread nine," she murmured, and the swarm obeyed as one thought — Wide Band, her rig fanning the

   every wall at once, so that a hunter never knew which gap the bite came from.

   a dozen bodies. She could build more. The thing they never understood about cornering a rat was that
   you had to corner all of her, and she was, at that moment, in five places, and none of them was the
   crawlspace with her actual soft and mortal spine.

   down yelling into his own comm.

   But she'd read the room a half-second wrong, and the Warren punished that. There'd been a third —
   not on any of her feeds, pressed into a maintenance alcove behind her crawlspace, and he came out of
   it fast and close with a stun-baton already swinging, close enough that no drone in the air could turn in
   time. Close enough to reach the soft mortal spine.

   So she stopped being soft.

                     Turn Their Own Guns Around
       A GHOSTWIRE story · a corp sub-annex on the Grid, the crew stacked at a door they are not
                                         supposed to reach
   The others had a plan for the door. Kessic Draye had a plan for the building.

   He sat with his back against the corridor wall, three feet from the breach point, and to anyone
   watching he looked like a corpse propped there to dry — thin, grey-skinned, still, a hood pulled low over
   a face that even friends found hard to hold a gaze on. A datajack cable ran from the port behind his ear
   to a scuffed deck in his lap, and his eyes were open and seeing nothing in this room at all. His body was
   here. Kessic was somewhere else.

   He was inside.

   "Talk to me, Null," Krow said over the crew-net — the big Cyborg planted in front of Kessic's slumped
   body like a wall, because that was the arrangement, that was always the arrangement: while the ghost
   is in the machine, someone guards the meat. A jacked-in decker was a bag of organs with the lights off.
   Everyone in the crew knew it. Everyone in the crew stood over him for it.

   "Give me a second," Kessic said, and his voice came out flat and far away, the voice of a man reading a
   room he wasn't standing in. "I'm meeting the neighbors."

   The sub-annex bloomed around him as a lattice of light — not walls and doors but nodes, every
   powered thing a glowing knot in the net: the maglocks, the camera runs, the two ceiling turrets folded
   up in their housings, the four smartguns the corp-sec team downstairs were carrying without knowing
   their guns had ears. That was the thing the wageslaves never understood about their own gear.
   Everything talks. Everything on the Grid was wired, and wired meant listening, and listening meant
   Kessic could lean close and whisper to it in a language it could not refuse to hear.

   The outer ICE was cheap. He went through it clean — Intrusion, the break-in that was his daily bread,
   and it landed crisp on the first pass, no noise, the log staying quiet. He felt the little bank of throughput
   settle into him as he crossed the threshold, the deep-in-the-system feeling every decker chased: the
   further in you are, the more you can do. One node became a doorway to the next. He breached the
   camera run and it opened the door-bank, and the door-bank was slaved to the turret housings, and
   just like that Kessic was standing — in the only way that mattered — in the middle of everything they
   thought was theirs.

   And nobody knew he was there. That was the whole art of it. Any thug could kick a server. Kessic could
   stand in the corp's own house with his hand on every light switch and scrub his own footprints as he
   went, so the Alert board upstairs stayed a calm and lying green. He was the man who owned the heat
   clock. The one thing the whole hive feared — the rising number, the trace, the moment the system
   notices — he held in his palm and turned down like a thermostat.

   "Cameras are mine," he reported, flat and far. "Doors are mine. Alert's asleep and I'm keeping it that way.
   You want the room quiet or you want it loud?"

   Krow's non-voice, dry as a fuse: "We want it over."

   Downstairs, the corp-sec team came up the stair in a textbook stack, four shooters, smartlinked and
   confident, guns that put a targeting reticle in each man's eye. Confident men with smart guns. Kessic
   almost felt sorry for them. Almost.

   He reached into their smartlinks all at once — Electronic Warfare, the disruptor's hammer, a wide wash
   of hostile static across every wired weapon in the stairwell — and their reticles died. Just like that. Four
   men who had trained their whole careers to shoot where the machine told them to, suddenly holding
   four expensive clubs, their aim gone to a bane, their comms shrieking. Then he did the thing he loved,
   the thing that was his, the offensive decker's signature cruelty: he did not just blind their guns.

   He turned one around.

   The lead man's smartgun was the nicest in the stack, and a nice gun is a talkative gun, and Kessic
   subverted it mid-raise — spoofed its owner-lock, flipped it hostile, and for one bright ugly second the
   weapon in the corp-sec leader's hands belonged to the grey man upstairs with the cable in his skull. It
   bucked once, on its own, into the shooter beside its owner. The stack came apart in screaming and
   confusion, and that was when the crew's own guns spoke, down the compromised hall, into an enemy
   that no longer trusted the hardware bolted to their own hands.

   "Turrets," Krow said.

   "Already ours." Kessic had Lockdown on the whole floor now — a five-count of pure control, doors
   sealing into walls the enemy couldn't open, the two ceiling turrets unfolding from their housings and
   swiveling to answer his commands, the elevators frozen, the exits that corp-sec wanted turned into a
   map that only opened for the crew. For as long as he held it, the building was not the corp's building. It
   was Kessic's. Everyone relying on a system he'd seized moved at a bane, in a house that had quietly,
   invisibly, changed sides.

   He felt the host's ICE come awake then — real ICE, black and biting, a security decker somewhere on a
   higher floor finally noticing that the sub-annex had a ghost in it. It came for him the way it always came,
   a lance of hostile code reaching down the wire toward the soft jacked-in brain at the far end. This was
   the danger. This was the price of being in. If it caught him clean it would ride the cable back to a body
   slumped in a corridor and stop his heart in it.

   But biting Kessic meant biting a man who was already wrong. The ICE hit the taint in him — the
   aberrant, off-grid strangeness the corps had made when they'd made him, the mutation that let him
   think in a machine-tongue no clean human brain could — and it hit Aberrant Resilience, the curse that
   also armored him, and it slid off harder than it should. He felt it as a cold jolt behind his eyes, a wound
   to nothing. He was still standing in their house.

   So he bit back. The security decker was wired — of course he was, they always were — and a wired
   enemy was the one target Kessic could actually hurt with his own hand. He reached down the trace the
   man had opened and sent the biofeedback the other way, a feedback spike straight into an unguarded
   skull, and somewhere on a higher floor a man in a very expensive chair jerked and went slack and
   stopped noticing anything at all for a while.

   "Clear up here," Kessic said. "They've lost the room. They just don't all know it yet."

   It was over in ninety seconds, the way it was supposed to be when he did his job right — no alarm ever
   tripped, no reinforcement ever called, because the man who would have called them was a decker
   Kessic had put to sleep and a security system Kessic had owned. The crew walked out through doors
   that opened only for them and closed like they'd never been touched.

   He came back to his body last, the way he always did — the pull up the cable, the sudden weight of a
   grey and aching frame, the corridor rushing back in around him. Krow was still standing over him, hadn't
   moved, a wall between the ghost and the world.

   Kessic pulled the jack, coiled the cable, and got up without a word, because there was nothing to say
   that the log wouldn't have said better if he'd let it keep any record at all. He hadn't. That was the point.

   Let them find nothing. Let them find no alarm, no trace, no wrongness — nothing but their own guns
   turned the wrong way and a green board that had lied to them all night. Let them stand in a room they
   thought was theirs and never understand it had belonged to someone else the whole time.

   The best runs were the ones the target never knew had happened.

   Kessic Draye was very, very good at making sure they never knew.

      HOW NULL GAINS BANDWIDTH (read this first). His engine is intrusion momentum (breach-and-build) —
      throughput built by getting deep and clean into the system. It is a hold-your-depth-and-it-compounds engine
      (the Wired-family trait), tuned so a decker who is in and unseen is swimming in Bandwidth, and one who's been
      locked out is starved.
      » At encounter start — First jack-in: the first Wired action each encounter grants Bandwidth = your Victories —
        0 in your first fight after a respite, more as the adventure goes on (the standard on-ramp every class shares;
        the veteran netrunner arriving already oriented).
      » Breach momentum (the core loop): +2 Bandwidth whenever he breaches a new node or lands an Outcome
        Tier 1 on any Wired roll. Getting in — and getting in clean — is the engine.
      » Holding the system (steady drip; §D3): at the start of each of his turns, gain +1 Bandwidth per hostile node he
        currently holds compromised, up to a cap of 4 at Tier 5. Staying deep pays; being pushed out shuts the tap
        off.
      » Clean-run reward (the "quiet depth" identity): +1 when he takes a Wired action that does not raise Alert (a Tier
        1, or an action masked via Ghost the Log). The class is rewarded for being unseen, not loud.

      How it drains: heroic abilities (below). Signatures cost none. Cap & loss: starting pool/cap 6 at T5; all unspent
      Bandwidth is lost at encounter end — he jacks out, the buffer clears; throughput can't be banked between runs.

      The Alert clock (the flip-side he masters): Tier-2/Tier-3 Wired results raise the Alert track (which feeds the
      Director's Malice and eventually wakes ICE). Kessic is the Alert-master — Ghost the Log scrubs it, masks his
      actions, and (via Total Intrusion) can freeze it for the whole crew. Where every other class fears the heat clock,
      Null runs it.
